# How Do Dental Implants Actually Work? The Four Parts, Explained

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A dental implant works by replacing the root of a missing tooth with a small titanium post that your jawbone grows onto and grips. That direct fusion of living bone to the implant surface is called osseointegration, and it is what turns a placed post into a fixed anchor. Once fusion has happened, a connector called an abutment is fitted to the top of the post, and a crown is attached to the abutment to do the visible work of a tooth. So an implant is really four things working together: the post, the abutment, the crown, and the bone that holds all three.

### Key Takeaways

- An implant is three manufactured components — post, abutment and crown — plus the living bone that grips them.
- Osseointegration is the direct bonding of living bone to the implant surface with no fibrous tissue in between, and it is the whole mechanism.
- Conventional healing before an implant carries a tooth is around three to four months in the lower jaw and six to eight months in the upper jaw.
- In a registry of 158,824 implants, the overall failure rate was 2.21%, and 1.56% failed early, during the fusion phase, before any crown was fitted.
- A meta-analysis put peri-implantitis at 19.53% of patients: an implant cannot decay, but the bone around it can still be lost.
- Bone volume decides the plan more than any other single factor, which is why some timelines run in months and others in weeks.

## Overview

If someone has told you an implant is an option, you have probably been shown a small diagram of a screw with a tooth on top and left to fill in the rest. The diagram is accurate as far as it goes, but it hides the part that does the work. The post does not wedge into a hole and stay there through friction. Your own bone has to grow onto it and lock it in place, and that biological process, not the hardware, decides whether the implant lasts a year or thirty.

This guide takes the mechanism apart in plain language. It covers the four parts an implant is built from, what osseointegration is and how it unfolds, why implant size and shape matter, what happens at each stage, how long treatment realistically takes, who is a candidate, how reliable implants are in large published series, and what can go wrong. Cost is a separate question with its own answer, covered in our guide to [what dental implants really cost in the East Bay](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay).

## What Are the Four Parts of a Dental Implant?

Most explanations count three parts, because three of them are things a manufacturer sells. The fourth is the one you already own. Leaving it out is what makes implants sound like carpentry rather than biology, and it is also why two people can be quoted very different treatment plans for what looks like the same missing tooth.

| Part | What It Is | What It Does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The fixture, or post | A small titanium or ceramic screw placed into the jawbone | Replaces the root and carries chewing force down into bone |
| The abutment | A connector that screws into the fixture and passes up through the gum | Joins the buried post to the visible tooth |
| The crown | A porcelain or zirconia tooth, made to match the ones beside it | The part you see, bite with and clean |
| The bone | Your own living jawbone | Grows onto the fixture and grips it; without enough of it, nothing above works |

### 1. The Fixture — the Replacement Root

The fixture is the part people mean when they say "the implant". It is a threaded post, most often titanium, placed into a socket prepared in the jaw. The [American Dental Association](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/implants) describes implants as being made of titanium and other materials compatible with the human body, surgically placed in the upper or lower jaw, where they function as a sturdy anchor for replacement teeth. Titanium is used because bone tolerates it unusually well and will grow directly onto its surface rather than walling it off, which is not true of most metals.

The fixture sits entirely below the gum while it heals, with nothing attached to it, because loading a post before it has fused is the main way an implant is lost early.

### 2. The Abutment — the Connector

The abutment is a short connector that screws into the top of the fixture and emerges through the gum. It exists because the post is buried and the tooth is not, and something has to bridge that gap while leaving a clean, cleanable margin at the gum line. Abutments are usually titanium or a ceramic such as zirconia, and the choice is partly aesthetic: a ceramic abutment does not cast a gray shadow through thin gum tissue at the front of the mouth. Fitting one is a small procedure rather than a surgical one.

### 3. The Crown — the Tooth You See

The crown is the visible tooth, made in a laboratory or milled in the practice to match the shade, shape and translucency of the teeth beside it. It either screws through the abutment or is cemented onto it, and you can see finished results in our [dental implant smile gallery](/smile-gallery/dental-implants).

A crown on an implant is not identical to a crown on a natural tooth, and the difference matters for the rest of your life with it. A natural tooth sits in a ligament that gives it a fraction of a millimeter of movement and a nerve supply that reports pressure. An implant is rigid in bone and has neither, so it will not ache to warn you that something is wrong. That is why the maintenance advice further down is not optional.

### 4. The Bone — the Part Nobody Counts

The fourth part is your jaw. An implant does not hold itself in; it is held. Bone volume and quality decide whether a fixture can be placed at all, how long healing takes, which size of implant can be used, and whether a graft has to come first.

This is also why timing matters after a tooth is lost. A systematic review of post-extraction dimensional changes in humans by Tan and colleagues, published in *Clinical Oral Implants Research*, found that alveolar ridge width can reduce by up to 50% in the first year after extraction, with roughly two-thirds of that loss happening in the first three months. The same review reported horizontal bone loss of 29% to 63% and vertical loss of 11% to 22% at six months. The bone that would have held an implant does not wait for you to decide. Where volume has already gone it can often be rebuilt, which is the purpose of [bone grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting), and in the upper back jaw, of [sinus augmentation](/procedures/periodontal-services/sinus-augmentation).

## What Is Osseointegration, and How Does It Actually Happen?

Osseointegration is the direct bonding and formation of bone against an implant surface without any layer of fibrous tissue in between. That definition comes from a 2026 review of implant osseointegration in the *Journal of Biomedical Materials Research Part A*, and the phrase "without intervening fibrous tissue" carries the meaning. If soft tissue forms between bone and metal instead of bone, the implant is mobile and it has failed, however solid it felt on the day it was placed.

### The Healing Sequence, Week by Week

The process is a bone-healing cascade and it follows a recognizable order. Blood fills the gap between implant and bone, platelets adhere to the surface and a clot forms, releasing growth factors. Over the following days immune cells arrive, new blood vessels grow, and stem cells turn into bone-forming cells that lay down a fast, disorganized early bone called woven bone. The [2026 osseointegration review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13312432/) describes bone establishing complete binding along the implant surface by around the fourth week, and that new bone being remodeled into mature lamellar bone by around the twelfth. The twelve-week mark is not marketing shorthand; it is roughly how long disorganized repair bone takes to be replaced by the organized, load-bearing kind.

Two kinds of stability are at play, and confusing them causes a lot of misunderstanding. Primary stability is mechanical: the grip the threads have the moment the implant is seated, set by the implant's shape and the density of the bone. Secondary stability is biological, and it is what osseointegration builds. A literature review in the *Journal of Dental Sciences* notes insertion torque in most studies ranged from 30 to 45 N·cm as the threshold for loading an implant immediately — another way of saying immediate work is only considered where the mechanical grip is already unusually good.

## Why Do Implant Size and Shape Matter?

Implants are not one object in one size. Diameter and length are chosen for the site, and the choice is usually forced by how much bone is available rather than by preference.

On diameter, a 2025 clinical trial in *Dentistry Journal* used the standard split: narrow-diameter implants are those under 3.75 mm, standard-diameter implants 3.75 mm and above. It recorded no failures in either group over 36 months and concluded [narrow implants had comparable clinical outcomes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12468807/) to standard ones supporting similar restorations.

On length, a 2021 prospective study in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* compared [short implants of 7 mm and 8.5 mm against standard lengths of 10 mm and above](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8199359/) across 99 implants in 74 patients. Survival was 100% in both groups at twelve months, and the authors concluded shorter implants can be as useful as standard-length ones in ridges where bone height has already been lost. That is worth knowing if you have been told there is not enough bone: a shorter or narrower fixture is sometimes the alternative to a graft.

## What Happens at Each Stage of the Process?

The sequence is consistent even though the timings are not. What varies is whether a tooth still has to come out, whether bone has to be rebuilt first, and which jaw the implant is going into.

| Stage | What Happens | How Long It Takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examination and imaging | Clinical examination plus a 3D scan to measure bone volume, height and the position of nerves and sinuses | One appointment |
| Extraction and grafting, only if needed | The failing tooth is removed, and bone is rebuilt where volume is short | Adds months before placement |
| Placing the fixture | The post is placed into the prepared site, usually under local anesthetic, and the gum is closed over it | Usually one appointment |
| Osseointegration | Nothing visible happens; bone grows onto the implant surface | Around 3–4 months lower jaw, 6–8 months upper |
| Abutment and impression | The fixture is uncovered, the abutment fitted, and an impression or scan taken for the crown | A short appointment |
| Fitting the crown | The finished tooth is attached and the bite adjusted | One appointment |
| Maintenance | Cleaning at home and professional monitoring of the gum and bone around the implant | Ongoing, for life |

The imaging stage is where the real answer to "can I have an implant" comes from. A [panoramic or 3D scan](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/panoramic-x-rays) shows bone height, bone width and the structures that have to be avoided, and no honest plan exists before it.

## How Long Does the Whole Implant Process Take?

The most commonly published range is three to nine months from placement to finished tooth, and it is wide because it covers two different jaws and several protocols. The conventional protocol is better defined than that range suggests. The Cochrane review *Interventions for replacing missing teeth: different times for loading dental implants* records that implants are traditionally kept [load-free for three to four months in mandibles and six to eight months in maxillae](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7156879/), which is where those numbers come from. At our practice, healing and integration typically take around six months, and longer where a graft comes first.

### Why the Upper Jaw Usually Takes Longer

The difference is bone density. The lower jaw is denser and grips a fixture more firmly on the day; the upper jaw, particularly toward the back, is softer and more porous, with the maxillary sinus above it. Softer bone means less primary stability at the start and a longer wait for the biology to catch up. An upper back implant taking twice as long as a lower front one is normal, not a sign that anything has gone wrong.

### What About Same-Day Teeth?

Immediate loading, meaning a temporary tooth attached at or near the time of placement, is real and it is not right for everyone. The same Cochrane review pooled 26 trials covering 1,217 participants and 2,120 implants and found no convincing evidence of a clinically important difference in prosthesis failure, implant failure or bone loss between different loading times, with no difference for immediate versus conventional loading in prosthesis failure (RR 1.90, 95% CI 0.67 to 5.34, 8 trials) or implant failure (RR 1.50, 95% CI 0.60 to 3.77, 10 trials). The honest reading is that immediate loading is not proven worse and not proven better, and the authors rated the evidence very low quality. In practice it depends on getting enough primary stability on the day, which makes it a decision taken in the chair rather than a promise made in advance.

## How Do Implants Work for More Than One Missing Tooth?

The mechanism does not change when more teeth are missing; the arithmetic does. Implants do not have to be placed one per tooth. Two fixtures can carry a three-unit bridge across a gap, and a full arch is commonly restored on four to six implants rather than the ten or twelve teeth the arch contains, because the bridge distributes load between the anchors.

This is where implants differ most clearly from the alternatives. A conventional [fixed bridge](/procedures/restoration/fixed-bridges) is carried by the teeth either side of the gap, which have to be reduced to hold it, and [dentures](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) sit on the gum and are carried by the ridge. An implant is the only one of the three that puts load back into bone, and bone that is loaded is bone that is maintained.

## Who Is a Candidate for a Dental Implant?

The [American Academy of Periodontology](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/dental-implant-procedures/) sets out three conditions for an ideal candidate: good general and oral health, adequate bone in the jaw to support the implant, and healthy gum tissue free of periodontal disease.

The third is the one most often skipped over. Active gum disease has to be treated and stabilized before an implant is placed, because the same bacteria and the same host response that destroyed bone around a natural tooth will do the same around an implant. If you are not sure whether that applies to you, [what periodontal disease actually is](/procedures/periodontal-services/what-is-periodontal-gum-disease) is the place to start.

On age, the ADA's position is refreshingly plain: if you are in good general health this treatment may be an option, and your health is more of a factor than your age. The ADA also notes chronic illnesses such as diabetes or leukemia may interfere with healing after surgery, and that tobacco use can slow it. Tooth loss is not only an older person's problem either — [NIDCR reports](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/research/data-statistics/tooth-loss/adults) that adults aged 20 to 64 have an average of 26 remaining teeth out of 32, and about 2% of that age group have none left.

## How Reliable Are Dental Implants Over Time?

Better than most dental work, and not perfectly. A large-scale electronic dental registry study published in the *Journal of Functional Biomaterials* in 2025 followed [158,824 implants placed in 53,874 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11856851/) between 2014 and 2022, which is a far larger evidence base than the single-practice figures usually quoted.

| What Was Measured | Result in the Registry |
| --- | --- |
| Overall implant failure rate | 2.21% |
| Overall survival | 97.79% |
| Early failure, during fusion and before any crown | 1.56% |
| Upper molar region | 3% |
| Central incisor region | 3.37% |
| Implants carrying a fixed restoration | 3.74% |
| Implants carrying a removable restoration | 9.32% |

Two things about that table need saying plainly. The last two rows are calculated on implants that reached the restorative stage, which is why they sit above the all-implant figure rather than beneath it, and comparing them directly against the 2.21% would mislead. The site rows describe roughly double the risk in the upper molar and central incisor regions than in other sites, which is what the bone-density explanation above would predict.

Failure rates declined markedly after the first year, matching the wider literature: implants are most vulnerable around the point at which they start carrying load, and comparatively stable afterwards. The registry's 97.79% overall survival is in line with the high long-term survival reported across large published series.

## What Can Go Wrong With a Dental Implant?

Two different things go wrong at two different times, and they have almost nothing to do with each other.

### Early Failure — Before the Crown Goes On

Early failure means the bone never fully bonded to the fixture, and in the registry above it accounted for 1.56% of all implants placed. Causes include insufficient primary stability at placement, contamination or overheating of the site during surgery, an implant loaded before it was ready, and healing impaired by something systemic. It is disappointing but usually recoverable: the site is allowed to heal, sometimes grafted, and a second attempt is often successful.

Smoking is the single largest modifiable risk here. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in [*Medicina*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8780868/), pooling 292 publications and more than 150,000 implants, found smokers had roughly 2.4 times the odds of implant failure (OR 2.402) and greater marginal bone loss than non-smokers. That is one study rather than settled consensus, and the size of the effect is striking enough to act on regardless.

### Peri-Implant Mucositis and Peri-Implantitis

Late failure is a gum and bone problem, not a hardware problem. The [American Academy of Periodontology](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/peri-implant-disease/) distinguishes two stages. Peri-implant mucositis is inflammation confined to the soft tissue with no bone loss, and it can be treated successfully and reversed if caught early. Peri-implantitis is inflammation plus deterioration of the bone supporting the implant, and it usually requires surgical treatment. The AAP names previous periodontal disease, poor plaque control, smoking and diabetes as the main risk factors, and red or tender gums around the implant, or bleeding when brushing, as the signs to act on.

A systematic review and meta-analysis in *BMC Oral Health* drawing on 57 studies put peri-implantitis at [19.53% at the patient level and 12.53% at the implant level](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9583568/). The authors add that prevalence came out higher in studies using probing depth as one of the diagnostic criteria, so the figure moves with how the disease is defined, which is why you will see other numbers quoted elsewhere; a more recent analysis using the World Workshop criteria reported 25.0% of patients. Either way, roughly one implant patient in five is a long way from "implants are permanent and need no attention".

## How Do You Look After an Implant?

An implant cannot get a cavity, because there is no living tooth structure to decay. The bone holding it can still be lost, so daily cleaning matters more around an implant than around a natural tooth, not less. Brushing and cleaning between the teeth removes the plaque that starts mucositis, the stage at which the problem is still reversible. Professional [periodontal maintenance](/procedures/periodontal-services/maintenance) adds what you cannot do at home: measuring the tissue around the implant and taking the occasional radiograph to confirm the bone level is holding steady.

If you grind your teeth, say so before the crown is designed. With no ligament to absorb force and no nerve to report overload, heavy grinding goes straight into bone and into the screw joint, so it is a design consideration rather than an afterthought. <details class="blog-faq"><summary>Is having an implant placed painful?</summary>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Can a dental implant get a cavity?</summary>

## Find Out What an Implant Would Involve in Your Case

The mechanism is the same for everyone; the plan is not. What decides your timeline, your options and whether a graft is part of the picture is the bone you have now, and that only becomes visible on an examination and a scan. If you are weighing an implant against a bridge or a denture, or you have been told there is not enough bone, that is a conversation worth having properly rather than from a diagram.
