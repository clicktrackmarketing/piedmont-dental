# Gum Disease Stages: From Gingivitis to Periodontitis (And When Each Is Reversible)

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

Gum disease moves through four recognized stages: gingivitis first, then early, moderate and advanced periodontitis. The line that matters falls between the first and the second. Gingivitis is inflammation of the gum tissue alone, and it is fully reversible with a professional cleaning and consistent home care. Once the disease crosses into periodontitis it has begun destroying the bone and attachment that hold your teeth in place, and that loss does not return on its own — periodontitis can be stopped and controlled for life, but not reversed.

### Key Takeaways

- Gingivitis is the only stage that genuinely reverses. Every stage after it is managed rather than cured.
- The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/data-research/facts-stats/fast-facts-gum-disease.html) reports that 42% of US adults aged 30 and older have periodontitis, and about 8% have the severe form.
- The crossing point is attachment and bone loss, not how sore your gums feel. Periodontitis is often painless.
- Your chart does not say "stage three". Clinicians use the American Academy of Periodontology's system, which assigns both a stage and a grade.
- Treatment escalates by stage: cleaning and home care, then deep cleaning below the gumline, then surgery.
- Once periodontitis has been treated, periodontal maintenance is the treatment. Stopping it is how the disease returns.

## Overview

Most people meet gum disease as a small, easily dismissed event: a pink rinse in the sink after brushing, gums that look puffy along one side, breath that does not freshen the way it used to. Nothing hurts, so nothing gets done — and that is precisely the window in which the condition is completely fixable. It closes quietly, without a symptom to mark the moment it does.

This guide sets out the four stages in plain language: what is happening in each one, where reversibility ends, how dentists actually classify the disease as opposed to how popular articles describe it, and which treatment belongs to which stage. It also covers what causes gum disease, who is most at risk, why it returns after treatment, and what the evidence does and does not say about its wider health effects.

## What Are the Four Stages of Gum Disease?

The four-stage description you will find on most health sites summarises a continuous process. Gum disease does not click from one stage into the next on a particular day; the tissue changes gradually as plaque sits undisturbed against the gumline and the body's inflammatory response does collateral damage. The stages name how far that process has traveled and what it will now take to stop it.

| Stage | What Is Happening | What You Might Notice | Reversible? | Usual Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Gingivitis | Inflammation of the gum tissue only. No bone or attachment lost. | Red or puffy gums, bleeding when brushing, bad breath. Usually no pain. | Yes, fully | Professional cleaning plus corrected home care |
| 2. Early periodontitis | Inflammation has passed below the gumline. Some attachment and bone lost; pockets forming. | Bleeding continues, gums start to pull back, persistent bad taste. Still often painless. | No — but arrestable | Scaling and root planing, then maintenance |
| 3. Moderate periodontitis | Deeper pockets, more bone gone, and the pockets now shelter bacteria brushing cannot reach. | Visible recession, sensitivity to cold, tenderness, a slightly different bite. | No | Scaling and root planing, often with pocket reduction surgery |
| 4. Advanced periodontitis | Substantial bone loss. The support around some teeth is failing. | Loose or shifting teeth, gaps opening, a changed bite, pus, discomfort. | No | Surgical and regenerative procedures; extraction where a tooth cannot be saved |

### Stage One: Gingivitis

Gingivitis is inflammation confined to the gum tissue, and the American Academy of Periodontology describes it plainly on its [gum disease information page](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/): the gums become red, swollen and bleed easily, and gingivitis is reversible with professional treatment and good at-home care. Nothing structural has been damaged — the bone and the fibres attaching gum to tooth are intact, and the swelling is your immune system responding to a film of bacteria left in place too long.

It is missed so consistently because it does not hurt. Bleeding gums are widely read as normal, or as a sign of brushing too hard, when in almost every case they signal inflammation. The ADA's [MouthHealthy](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/gum-disease) guidance is direct about the remedy: gingivitis can usually be eliminated by a professional cleaning followed by daily brushing and flossing. That is the whole treatment.

### Stage Two: Early Periodontitis

Early periodontitis is the point at which inflammation moves below the gumline and starts consuming the attachment holding gum against tooth root, along with a small amount of the bone underneath. The gum separates from the tooth, and that separation creates a pocket — a space deeper than brush or floss can reach, which then collects exactly the bacteria that caused it.

This is where the honest answer to "can I fix this at home?" changes to no. Better brushing improves the inflammation at the top, but it cannot reach the base of a pocket, and bone that has gone does not grow back because you started flossing. What treatment does from here is stop the loss and hold it there — a good outcome, and not the same thing as reversal.

### Stage Three: Moderate Periodontitis

By the moderate stage the process has become self-sustaining: the pocket shelters the bacteria, the bacteria drive the inflammation, and the inflammation deepens the pocket around more teeth. Recession usually becomes visible now, and teeth can turn sensitive to cold as root surfaces that were never designed to be exposed meet air and drink. Many people here still describe their gums as fine, which is not denial — periodontitis is genuinely painless for a large proportion of patients until it is well advanced.

### Stage Four: Advanced Periodontitis

Advanced periodontitis is defined by how much support has been lost rather than by how bad the gums look. Enough bone is gone that the teeth begin to move: they loosen, drift, and change how the bite fits together. The AAP notes that once the bone supporting the teeth has been destroyed, teeth can become loose and may fall out or need to be removed. Treatment here is about saving what can be saved, often mixing periodontal surgery with restorative work. Advanced does not mean hopeless, but the range of outcomes is narrower than it was three stages earlier.

## Why Is Gingivitis Reversible and Periodontitis Not?

Because they damage different things. Gingivitis is inflammation of soft tissue that returns to normal once the irritant is removed; take the plaque away and the gum settles and reattaches as it was. Periodontitis destroys the periodontal attachment and the bone around the tooth, and neither rebuilds spontaneously when the area is cleaned. Removing the cause stops further loss, which is why treatment works, but it does not restore what has gone.

This is the point most articles blur, usually by saying "the earlier you catch it, the more reversible it is." Reversibility is not a sliding scale. It is a property of one stage.

### What "Manageable" Actually Means

Manageable is a stronger word than it sounds. Periodontitis that is treated and then properly maintained can stay stable for decades, with the same teeth, no further bone loss and no symptoms. It does not have to progress simply because it exists — it requires only that maintenance continues, because the conditions that produced it have not gone anywhere.

There is one honest exception, and it cuts against the flat "irreversible" line. The AAP notes that regenerative procedures can reverse some of the damage by regenerating lost bone and tissue, using membranes, bone grafts or tissue-stimulating proteins, and that work is described on the practice's [bone grafting page](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting). But it is site-specific surgery on selected defects rather than a general undo — it recovers some of what was lost in particular places, not the original architecture across the mouth.

## How Do Dentists Decide Which Stage You Are In?

Not by looking. Staging comes from a periodontal examination, in which the gum is measured at several points around every tooth to find where the attachment now sits, combined with x-rays showing the level of the bone. The measurements matter less as individual numbers than as a map: how many sites are affected, how deep the worst are, and whether any teeth have already been lost. Two people can have identically red gums and land in different stages, because what is assessed is structural loss rather than appearance. The practice's [periodontal treatment page](/procedures/periodontal-services/treatment) sets out what the assessment involves.

### Stage Describes Damage, Grade Describes Speed

The system clinicians actually use is the classification agreed at the 2017 World Workshop and published by the American Academy of Periodontology and the European Federation of Periodontology, which the AAP refers to when it notes that periodontitis is classified by stage and grade. It assigns two things rather than one. The stage, from I to IV, records how much damage exists and how complex the case is to treat. The grade, from A to C, records how fast the disease appears to be progressing and how well it is likely to respond to standard treatment.

The grade is the part patients never hear about, and it is arguably the more useful half. Two people can both be Stage III, with the same bone loss on the same x-rays, and face completely different futures: one progressing slowly with no risk factors, the other rapidly with poorly controlled diabetes or a heavy smoking history. It is also why the popular lists disagree with your chart — they are consumer summaries of that framework, which assigns one stage and one grade per patient rather than a mix. Background on the condition sits on the practice's [what is periodontal gum disease](/procedures/periodontal-services/what-is-periodontal-gum-disease) page.

## How Do You Know Which Stage You Are In at Home?

You mostly do not, and that is the honest answer. What you can do is notice whether any warning sign is present at all, because that is the trigger to have it looked at, whichever stage it represents. The ADA's MouthHealthy lists the signs:

- Gums that bleed easily when you brush or floss
- Red, swollen or tender gums
- Gums that have pulled away from the teeth
- Persistent bad breath or a bad taste that does not clear
- Loose or separating teeth
- A change in the way your teeth fit together when you bite
- A change in the fit of a partial denture

Two of these are more informative than the rest. Bleeding on its own most often means gingivitis, the good-news case. Teeth that have loosened or moved almost never mean gingivitis, because gingivitis does not touch the structures that hold teeth still — so if your bite has changed or a tooth feels mobile, the process has been running below the gumline for some time.

## What Causes Gum Disease in the First Place?

The starting point is dental plaque, a soft biofilm of bacteria that forms continuously on tooth surfaces and along the gumline. Left in place it hardens into calculus, which is rough, porous, firmly attached and impossible to remove with a brush. Calculus then holds fresh plaque against the gum permanently, and the immune system's sustained response to that produces the redness, the bleeding and eventually the breakdown of the tissue around it.

Two things follow that are easy to miss. The damage in periodontitis is largely done by your own inflammatory response rather than by the bacteria directly, which is why two people with similar hygiene can have very different outcomes. And once calculus has formed below the gumline, no amount of improved home care removes it — that is the specific job of scaling and root planing. Technique still decides whether it forms at all, and the practice's guide to [brushing and flossing properly](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/how-to-properly-brush-floss) covers what works.

## Who Is Most Likely to Develop It?

Plaque is necessary but not sufficient. Whether it progresses beyond gingivitis, and how quickly, depends on a set of risk factors the AAP sets out on its [gum disease risk factors](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/gum-disease-risk-factors/) page.

- **Age.** The AAP reports that over 70% of Americans aged 65 and older have some form of gum disease.
- **Tobacco use.** The AAP calls it possibly the most significant risk factor in both the development and the progression of periodontal disease.
- **Genetics.** Some people are more susceptible, and develop disease despite good habits.
- **Stress.** It makes it harder for the body to fight off infection.
- **Medications.** Oral contraceptives, some antidepressants and certain heart medicines can affect gum health.
- **Clenching or grinding.** Excess force does not cause gum disease, but it can speed up how fast the tissues are destroyed.
- **Other systemic conditions.** Cardiovascular disease, diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis are all associated with it.
- **Nutrition and obesity.** A diet low in key nutrients compromises immune response, and obesity may increase risk.

If several apply to you, the sensible response is not alarm but a shorter interval between periodontal examinations. Risk determines how often the disease should be looked for, not whether you will get it.

## How Is Each Stage Treated?

Treatment escalates with the stage, and each level reaches something the previous one cannot. The whole ladder is available at the practice, and the [periodontal services](/procedures/periodontal-services) section describes each procedure in detail.

| Stage | Primary Treatment | What It Achieves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gingivitis | Professional cleaning plus corrected home care | Full resolution — the gum returns to health |
| Early periodontitis | Scaling and root planing, then maintenance | Halts progression; pockets shrink as inflammation resolves |
| Moderate periodontitis | Scaling and root planing, often with pocket reduction surgery | Reaches deposits instruments could not otherwise clear, and makes pockets cleanable |
| Advanced periodontitis | Surgical and regenerative procedures; extraction where a tooth is not restorable | Stabilizes what can be saved and rebuilds selected defects |

### Where Deep Cleaning Starts

Scaling and root planing is the first treatment that goes below the gumline, and it is the standard response once periodontitis has been diagnosed. The AAP's [non-surgical treatments](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/periodontal-treatments-and-procedures/non-surgical-treatments/) page describes it as scaling beneath the gumline to remove plaque and bacterial toxins from the periodontal pockets, after which the root surface is smoothed so the gum has a clean surface to reattach against. The practice's approach is set out on the [scaling and root planing](/procedures/periodontal-services/periodontal-scaling-root-planing) page. The AAP also notes that controlled studies have found similar results from laser treatment compared with specific other non-surgical options — worth knowing if a laser is presented as a categorically different treatment.

### Where Surgery Starts

Surgery enters when non-surgical treatment has not achieved periodontal health, which is exactly how the AAP frames it. In pocket reduction surgery the gum tissue is folded back so disease-causing bacteria can be removed and bone irregularities smoothed, allowing the tissue to reattach to healthy bone. Where bone has been destroyed, regenerative procedures using membranes, bone grafts or tissue-stimulating proteins may rebuild some of it. Recession is a related but distinct problem with its own path, and exposed roots are sometimes covered with a graft — the practice's [gum grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/gum-grafting) page covers that.

### One Treatment to Be Careful About

Tray delivery systems — custom trays used to apply a medication to the gums at home — are marketed in some places as an alternative to conventional periodontal treatment. The AAP's position on its non-surgical treatments page is unusually blunt: the FDA clearance process did not determine that any specific medication delivered via tray delivery systems has been proven to be a safe or effective way to treat gum disease. A tray is not a substitute for removing what sits under the gum.

## Can Gum Disease Come Back After Treatment?

Yes, and expecting it to is the correct posture. The AAP notes that while many patients need no further treatment after the initial procedures, most require ongoing maintenance to preserve periodontal health. Treatment resolves the inflammation; it does not change the fact that plaque re-forms daily, that reduced pockets are still deeper than normal gum, or that whatever risk factors you carry are still yours.

Periodontal maintenance is therefore not a cleaning with a different name. It runs on a shorter interval than a routine recall, includes re-measuring the gums so any site starting to slip is caught early, and continues indefinitely — which is what the practice's [periodontal maintenance](/procedures/periodontal-services/maintenance) page describes. The most common reason periodontitis progresses after successful treatment is that maintenance stopped.

## Does Gum Disease Affect the Rest of Your Health?

There are well-documented associations, and it is worth being precise about them. The AAP's page on [gum disease and other diseases](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/gum-disease-and-other-diseases/) states directly that a causal relationship has not been conclusively established, while research suggests periodontal disease may contribute to the progression of other conditions. That qualifier matters, and a great deal of writing on this subject drops it.

Within that framing the associations are real. People with diabetes are more likely to have periodontal disease, probably because they are more susceptible to infection, and the relationship appears to run both ways. Several studies have found periodontal disease may increase the risk of heart disease, with inflammation the suspected mechanism, and links have been reported with respiratory disease, certain cancers and Alzheimer's disease. Treating gum disease is worth doing for what it does to your teeth, which is certain; any wider benefit is a bonus rather than the argument.

## How Do You Stop It Getting Worse?

Prevention and maintenance are the same activity at different points on the curve: disrupt plaque daily, and have removed professionally what you cannot reach. Brushing twice a day and cleaning between the teeth once a day handles the first part; a regular examination and cleaning handles the second, and catches the changes you cannot feel — the practice's [exams and cleanings](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/dental-exams-cleanings) page sets out what a visit involves. Beyond that, two things matter more than any product: stopping smoking, given how heavily the AAP weights tobacco, and telling your dental team about the rest of your health, since diabetes, medications, pregnancy or a stretch of high stress all change how often your gums should be assessed. If a general dentist finds disease beyond what a hygienist's cleaning will address, the next step is a periodontal assessment, and it is worth asking for one rather than waiting to be offered it.

Two related questions come up constantly alongside staging. If you are wondering whether your case has moved past what a regular cleaning can handle, see [when to see a gum specialist](/blog/when-to-see-a-gum-specialist). <details class="blog-faq"><summary>How do I know if I have gingivitis or periodontitis?</summary>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>How long does it take for gingivitis to become periodontitis?</summary>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Is a deep cleaning the same as a regular cleaning?</summary>
<p>No. A routine cleaning works at and just above the gumline; scaling and root planing goes beneath it to remove deposits and bacterial toxins from inside the periodontal pockets, then smooths the root surface. It treats diagnosed periodontitis, and is not an upgrade to a standard cleaning.</p>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Is gum disease contagious?</summary>
<p>The bacteria involved can transfer between people through saliva, but the disease itself is not simply caught. ## Find Out Which Stage You Are Actually At

Almost every question on this page resolves to one thing: whether the inflammation has stayed above the gumline or moved below it. That is not something you can judge from a mirror, and it does not hurt enough to announce itself. One periodontal examination settles it, and the answer decides whether you are looking at a cleaning and a change of habit or a treatment plan.
