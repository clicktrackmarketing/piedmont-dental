# What a Dental Cleaning Costs in the East Bay

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A routine cleaning appointment is almost never billed as one charge. It is three separate line items — the cleaning, the examination and any X-rays — which is why the total on the statement rarely matches the number people had in their head. For patients without dental benefits, [Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/us/en/protect-my-smile/procedures/dental-cleanings/cost-and-insurance-coverage.html) publishes a national range of $85 to $160 for a standard cleaning on its own, while [CareCredit](https://www.carecredit.com/dentistry/costs/) puts the national average for a routine exam that includes a cleaning and X-rays at $203, with California at $206. If you have a dental plan, preventive care is the one category most plans cover at or near 100 percent.

### Key Takeaways

- A cleaning visit is billed as three items: cleaning, exam and X-rays.
- Delta Dental publishes $85 to $160 for a standard cleaning without benefits.
- CareCredit's national average for exam, cleaning and X-rays is $203; California is $206.
- First visits cost more because a new patient gets a comprehensive evaluation and baseline X-rays.
- Most dental plans cover preventive care at 100 percent, typically twice a year.
- A deep cleaning is a different procedure, priced per quadrant and covered under a different tier.

## Overview

Most people book a cleaning expecting a single, modest, predictable charge, then find a statement with three or four lines on it and no obvious way to tell whether the number is normal. It is an uncomfortable position, because the vocabulary on the bill is not the vocabulary anyone used in the chair, and the honest answer to "what will this cost?" genuinely depends on things nobody knows until you are examined. That does not make the number unknowable in advance. It means the question has to be asked in the right shape.

This guide breaks a routine visit into the parts that are actually billed, gives published market ranges for each with the source named, and explains why the first appointment costs more than every one that follows. It covers what dental insurance typically pays for preventive care and where that generosity stops, why a deep cleaning is priced on a completely different basis, and what genuinely moves the figure on your own estimate. Every number here is a published third-party range, not a quote — only an examination produces a real one.

## Why Does One Cleaning Visit Produce Three Charges?

Dentistry is billed in procedure codes, not appointments. The system is the Code on Dental Procedures and Nomenclature, or CDT, which the [American Dental Association](https://www.ada.org/resources/practice/dental-insurance/frequently-asked-questions-regarding-dental-codes) maintains through its Council on Dental Benefit Programs' Code Maintenance Committee and updates annually. Every distinct thing done to you in that hour has its own code, and every code carries its own fee and its own insurance treatment. A cleaning appointment is a bundle of separate procedures that happen to be scheduled together, which is exactly why the total surprises people.

It does not help that the codes use clinical terminology where patients use plain English. The ADA's own [dental treatment terms](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/dental-care/dental-treatment-terms) page exists to translate them: prophylaxis means teeth cleaning, radiographs mean X-rays, and scaling and root planing means deep cleaning. If you can read your statement in those three translations, most of the confusion disappears.

### The Cleaning Itself

The cleaning is the prophylaxis — the removal of plaque, calculus and stains from the tooth surfaces, usually done by a hygienist rather than a dentist. It is a preventive procedure, billed once regardless of how long it takes, and the code that applies depends on dentition rather than birthday. The ADA is explicit that "dentition, not age, is the criterion for reporting an adult versus child prophylaxis," which is why a fifteen-year-old with a full set of adult teeth is billed as an adult and their parent notices the change.

Delta Dental publishes a national range of $85 to $160 for a standard cleaning without dental benefits. That figure is the cleaning alone, and reading it as the price of the visit is the single most common source of sticker shock.

### The Examination

The examination is a separate code performed by the dentist, and it is not a formality bolted onto the cleaning. It is the appointment's diagnostic half — checking for decay, assessing the gums, looking at existing restorations and screening the soft tissues. There are two different evaluation codes depending on your history with the practice, and which one you get is the main reason a first visit and a sixth visit produce different totals.

### The X-rays

Radiographs are billed by type and by how many images are taken, and the spread between the cheapest and the most expensive option is wide. Using CareCredit's national cost data, a bitewing X-ray averages $65, a panoramic image $200, and a full-mouth series $226. Which of those you need is a clinical decision rather than a default. The ADA's guidance on [X-rays](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/x-rays) is blunt about it: "You may not need an X-ray at every dental visit," and under the 2024 ADA recommendations they "should be taken only when your dentist believes they will provide the necessary diagnostic information." How often you need them depends on your present oral health, your age, your risk for disease, and any signs or symptoms of oral disease. You can read more about the types on the practice's [dental X-rays](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/dental-x-rays) page.

## What Does a Routine Cleaning Visit Cost?

The table below sets out published third-party ranges for the procedures that make up a preventive visit. The two sources measure different things — Delta Dental prices individual procedures for patients without benefits, while CareCredit's figures come from the 2024 Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study conducted by ASQ360° Market Research and describe a bundled routine visit. Both are shown as they were published rather than blended into one number.

| Line item | Published figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard cleaning (prophylaxis), no benefits | $85 – $160 | Delta Dental |
| Routine exam including cleaning and X-rays | $203 national average, $50 – $350 range | CareCredit / Synchrony 2024 study |
| Same routine exam, California average | $206 | CareCredit / Synchrony 2024 study |
| Bitewing X-ray | $65 | CareCredit / Synchrony 2024 study |
| Panoramic X-ray | $200 | CareCredit / Synchrony 2024 study |
| Full-mouth X-ray series | $226 | CareCredit / Synchrony 2024 study |

Read together, those rows explain the arithmetic. CareCredit describes its $203 national figure as the cost of "a routine dental exam, which includes a full dental cleaning and X-rays" — a bundle, not a line item — while Delta Dental's $85 to $160 is the cleaning by itself. Neither is wrong. They are answers to different questions, and most of the confusion about dental pricing comes from comparing one to the other as though they were the same thing.

The wider context is that dental fees have been rising steadily. The ADA Health Policy Institute's [State of the U.S. Dental Economy](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market/state-of-the-us-dental-economy) reports fee growth of roughly 3 to 4 percent year over year, broadly in step with general inflation, which is worth knowing if you are comparing a figure you were quoted three years ago.

## Why Does the First Visit Cost More Than the Ones After It?

Because a first visit is a different procedure. A new patient is not simply having their teeth cleaned; they are being assessed from scratch, and the record that assessment creates is what every future appointment is measured against. Two things drive the difference — the evaluation code and the X-rays — and both are legitimate rather than an upsell.

### The Two Evaluation Codes, and Which One You Get

The ADA describes the distinction plainly. Code D0150 is the "comprehensive oral evaluation — new or established patient," and it "is used for new patients, established patients who have experienced a significant change in health conditions or established patients who have been absent from active treatment for three or more years." Code D0120, the "periodic oral evaluation," is the one "used for established patients returning to determine any changes in their dental and medical health status since a previous comprehensive or periodic evaluation."

In practical terms, the comprehensive evaluation is longer and more thorough, so it carries a higher fee than the periodic one you will be billed at subsequent visits. It is also the reason a long gap between appointments can reset you: three or more years away from active treatment puts you back into the comprehensive code, whether or not you consider yourself a new patient.

### The Records Question Most People Never Ask

The other half of the first-visit premium is imaging. A new dentist has nothing to compare today's mouth against, so, as the ADA puts it, "if you are a new patient, your dentist may recommend X-rays to determine your current oral health and have a baseline to help identify changes that may occur later." That is a real clinical need, not a formality.

There is, though, a step most patients never take. The same ADA guidance notes that where prior images exist, the new dentist may request copies from your previous practice instead of ordering new ones. Requesting your records before your first appointment costs nothing, takes one phone call, and can remove a line item worth more than the cleaning itself. It will not always be possible — images may be too old, or may not show what needs to be seen — but it is worth asking before you assume a full series is unavoidable.

## What Does Dental Insurance Usually Cover at 100 Percent?

Preventive care is the part of dentistry that insurance treats most generously, and that is deliberate. Delta Dental describes the standard structure as covering "100% of routine preventive and diagnostic care such as cleanings and exams," 80 percent of basic procedures such as fillings, root canals and tooth extractions, and 50 percent of major work such as crowns, bridges and implants. On its [preventive care](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/oral-health-habits/preventive-dental-care/) page it goes further: preventive services "are often covered at 100 percent with minimal or no deductibles or co-pays," and "with some plans, preventive services are not counted against your annual maximum."

Those two sentences are the most financially useful thing in this article. Where they apply to your plan, a cleaning and exam costs you nothing at the desk and does not eat into the pot of money you might need later for a filling — in plain terms, a benefit you have already paid for through your premiums and may as well use. The frequency Delta Dental describes as typical is "generally two per year," with the caveat to talk to your dentist about the right interval for your own mouth — and whether twice a year is actually right for you is a question your dentist can answer from your own gum health and risk factors.

### Where the Hundred Percent Stops

The generous tier applies to preventive and diagnostic care as the plan defines it, and plan definitions vary. X-rays sit in the diagnostic category and are often covered, but coverage is commonly limited to a set number per period. A cleaning that becomes a treatment — because the examination finds gum disease — leaves the preventive tier entirely and is paid at a lower percentage, usually after a deductible. Cosmetic work sits outside the structure altogether.

The practical consequence is that "my cleanings are covered" is true right up until the day it is not, and the day it is not is usually the day something is found. That is a coverage boundary rather than a billing trick, but it lands like a surprise if nobody has explained it.

### Frequency Limits, Waiting Periods and the Annual Maximum

Three plan mechanics decide what you actually pay. A waiting period is, in Delta Dental's words, "the period of time before you are eligible to receive benefits for all or certain dental treatments" — relevant if you have just changed jobs. An annual maximum is "the total amount your dental insurance will pay for your coverage during a 12-month period," though Delta Dental notes only 2 to 4 percent of Americans typically exceed theirs. And plans commonly enforce time limits between certain services and frequency limits on procedures like X-rays.

The frequency limits are where preventive patients get caught. If your plan allows two cleanings per benefit year and you take one in January and one in November, a December appointment falls outside the allowance even though it feels like a normal interval. Booking against your plan's calendar rather than your own is a small piece of admin that saves real money. The practice's [insurance information](/resources/insurance) page is the place to start with your own plan's specifics, and your plan documents remain the only authority on what your particular contract pays.

## Why Is a Deep Cleaning Priced Differently?

A deep cleaning is not a more thorough version of a routine cleaning. It is a treatment for diagnosed gum disease, it works below the gumline rather than above it, and it is billed on an entirely different basis — per quadrant, meaning one quarter of the mouth at a time. That single fact accounts for most of the shock when a patient who expected a cleaning is quoted a figure several times larger.

| Periodontal procedure | Published range without benefits | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scaling and root planing | $180 – $295 per quadrant | One quarter of the mouth |
| Periodontal maintenance | $140 – $220 | The shorter-interval visit after treatment |
| Full mouth debridement | $150 – $260 | Clearing heavy deposit so an exam is possible |

Those ranges are Delta Dental's published figures for patients without dental benefits. CareCredit's national data puts scaling and root planing at $242 per quadrant, which sits inside Delta Dental's range — the two sources broadly agree, and both are per quadrant. A mouth needing all four quadrants treated is therefore four times the line, and the maintenance visits that follow sit above the price of the routine cleaning you were used to. Because it is a treatment rather than prevention, it also moves into the lower coverage tier.

If you have been told you need one and want to understand the reasoning before you agree, a separate comparison of [deep cleaning versus regular cleaning](/blog/deep-cleaning-vs-regular-cleaning) sets out how the decision is made and what the evidence says it achieves. You can also read about [scaling and root planing](/procedures/periodontal-services/periodontal-scaling-root-planing) directly.

## Does the East Bay Cost More Than the National Average?

Honestly, there is no published dataset that breaks dental fees down to Piedmont, Oakland or the East Bay, and any article that gives you a precise local figure has either surveyed practices itself or invented it. What does exist is state-level data, and it is less dramatic than most people expect. The 2024 Synchrony study behind CareCredit's figures puts the average routine dental exam in California at $206 against a national average of $203, with the highest state at $263 and the lowest at $153.

That is a difference of a few dollars at the state level, which sits oddly against the everyday experience of Bay Area prices. Two things reconcile it. A statewide average blends San Francisco with the Central Valley, so it flattens exactly the variation a local reader is asking about. And the bigger driver of what you personally pay is not geography at all — it is which procedures you need, whether you are new, and how your plan is written. A first visit with a full series of X-rays will cost more than a routine recall visit in the same chair, in any city in the country.

What is fair to say about a high-cost area is that practice overheads — rent, wages, supplies and compliance — are real and are rising, which is the pressure the ADA Health Policy Institute describes across the profession. What is not fair to say is that any specific East Bay premium has been measured, because no published dataset appears to measure it.

## What Actually Changes the Number on Your Own Estimate?

Four variables explain almost all the difference between one patient's total and another's at the same practice on the same day.

### 1. Whether You Are a New Patient

The comprehensive evaluation and baseline imaging make a first appointment the most expensive preventive visit you will have. This is a one-off, not a running rate.

### 2. Which X-rays Are Taken

The gap between a set of bitewings and a full-mouth series is the widest single swing in a preventive visit — using CareCredit's figures, $65 against $226. This is a clinical decision based on your history and risk, and it is a fair thing to ask about before the images are taken.

### 3. Whether Your Gums Need Treatment Rather Than Prevention

If the examination and charting find pocketing that meets the criteria for periodontal treatment, the appointment stops being a preventive one. The procedure changes, the per-quadrant basis applies, and the insurance tier changes with it.

Beyond those three, the fourth variable is your plan itself — whether the practice you choose is in-network for it, what your deductible is, and how your benefit year is defined. Two patients having identical treatment can pay very different amounts for that reason alone, and neither figure tells you anything about whether the fee was reasonable.

## How Do You Get a Reliable Number Before You Sit Down?

Ask for a written estimate with the procedure codes on it, not a verbal ballpark. Codes are national, so a plan can price them exactly, and an estimate listing D0150, a prophylaxis and a specific radiograph is a document your insurer can act on in a way that "a cleaning and checkup" is not. If any significant work is proposed, send that estimate to your plan for a pre-treatment estimate before you agree to it — the insurer tells you in writing what it will pay, which removes the guesswork entirely.

Two questions are worth asking when you book rather than when you arrive. Ask which evaluation code applies to you, since that tells you immediately whether you are being seen as new or established. And ask whether your previous X-rays can be transferred instead of retaken. Neither question is confrontational, both have straightforward answers, and together they cover the two line items most likely to make a first bill larger than expected.

## What If You Do Not Have Dental Benefits?

You are not unusual. The National Association of Dental Plans reported in its [2025 Dental Benefits Report](https://www.nadp.org/nadp-report-shows-continued-decline-in-dental-benefits-enrollment/) that nearly 284 million Americans — 83 percent of the population — had some form of dental benefit in 2024, which leaves 13 percent with no dental coverage at all. Enrollment has been falling rather than rising.

Without a plan you are paying the published fee rather than a negotiated one, so the self-pay ranges in this article are the relevant ones, and the first visit is still the most expensive preventive appointment you will have. The practice's [insurance information](/resources/insurance) and [cleanings and prevention](/procedures/cleanings-prevention) pages are the places to start, and asking for the written, coded estimate matters more when there is no plan absorbing part of the bill.
