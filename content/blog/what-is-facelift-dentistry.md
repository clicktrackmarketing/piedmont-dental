# What Is "Facelift Dentistry"? The Full-Mouth Rejuvenation Trend, Explained

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

"Facelift dentistry" is a marketing term, not a recognized dental specialty. The National Commission on Recognition of Dental Specialties and Certifying Boards recognizes twelve dental specialties, and neither facelift dentistry nor cosmetic dentistry nor anti-aging dentistry is among them. What sits underneath the name is real, established work: full-mouth rehabilitation, in which worn or missing teeth are rebuilt to a restored height so the lower face regains the tooth support it has lost. The published change is measured in millimeters, it treats teeth and bite only, and it is not a substitute for surgery.

### Key Takeaways

- "Facelift dentistry" began as one practice's branded name for its program, not a recognized category of dental care.
- The underlying treatment is full-mouth rehabilitation, and the parameter being changed is occlusal vertical dimension — the height your teeth hold your jaws apart at.
- In published long-term cases, that height was increased by 2 to 3 millimeters, not more.
- Severe tooth wear rises with age, from a predicted 3% of adults at 20 to 17% at 70 in a systematic review, so genuine candidates do exist.
- The American Society of Plastic Surgeons states that a facelift "can only be performed surgically" and that minimally invasive treatments cannot achieve the same results.
- If your teeth are not worn, there is no lost height to give back, and the case for treatment is much weaker.

## Overview

You have probably arrived here because you saw a set of before-and-after photographs with a caption promising a facelift without surgery, and you want to know whether that is a genuine dental treatment or a clever piece of advertising. It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that it is a little of both. The dentistry underneath is real and long-established. The name attached to it is a marketing construction, and the promise it borrows from plastic surgery is considerably larger than what the dental work delivers.

This guide separates the two. It covers where the term comes from, what is actually being treated when a dentist talks about rebuilding your bite, what the published research says about how much change is achievable and how long it holds, which procedures make up a full-mouth plan, who is a genuine candidate, what it costs as a market range, and the questions worth asking before you agree to anything irreversible.

## Is Facelift Dentistry a Recognized Dental Specialty?

No, and that matters more than it sounds, because the phrase is often presented as though it names a discipline with its own training pathway and board examination. Dental specialties in the United States are recognized through a formal process, and a practitioner who has completed one has done an accredited postgraduate residency in it — three additional years for a periodontist, for example, according to the American Academy of Periodontology.

### The Twelve Recognized Specialties

The [National Commission on Recognition of Dental Specialties and Certifying Boards](https://ncrdscb.ada.org/recognized-dental-specialties), which operates under the American Dental Association, recognizes exactly twelve: dental anesthesiology, dental public health, endodontics, oral and maxillofacial pathology, oral and maxillofacial radiology, oral and maxillofacial surgery, oral medicine, orofacial pain, orthodontics and dentofacial orthopedics, pediatric dentistry, periodontics, and prosthodontics. Facelift dentistry is not on that list. Neither is cosmetic dentistry, nor anti-aging dentistry, nor any of the branded variations you will encounter.

The specialty that genuinely covers this territory is prosthodontics, which the Commission defines as the discipline concerned with "the diagnosis, treatment planning, rehabilitation and maintenance of the oral function, comfort, appearance and health of patients with clinical conditions associated with missing or deficient teeth." That is full-mouth rehabilitation described in plain terms, and it existed long before anyone attached a cosmetic-surgery metaphor to it.

### Where the Term Actually Comes From

The phrase is not a clinical term. It started as the branded name one Southern California practice uses for its own program. Related phrasings — dental facelift, non-surgical facelift dentistry, full-mouth rejuvenation — have since been adopted much more widely by practices with no connection to it.

None of that makes the underlying dentistry improper. It does mean that when you read the term, you are reading a brand name or an imitation of one, not a procedure code, and that two practices using the same phrase may be describing quite different treatment plans. Similarly, any licensed general dentist may perform this work and describe it as cosmetic dentistry, because that is a description of the treatment rather than a protected credential. Judge the plan you are shown, not the label on it — our guide on [how to choose a cosmetic dentist in the East Bay](/blog/how-to-choose-cosmetic-dentist-east-bay) sets out the checks worth running, and the range of work involved is described across the [cosmetic dentistry pages](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry).

## What Is Actually Being Treated Here?

Strip the marketing away and a specific, measurable dental parameter sits underneath: occlusal vertical dimension. It is the height at which your teeth hold your upper and lower jaws apart when you bite together, and it sets the distance between your nose and your chin. Teeth are load-bearing structures for the lower face, and their height is what supports the lips and the surrounding soft tissue from the inside.

### How Teeth Lose Height

Enamel does not regrow. Decades of chewing, grinding, and acid exposure from diet or reflux wear the biting surfaces down, and in some people that wear is fast and severe. Grinding is a substantial contributor: a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of global bruxism prevalence put the overall figure at roughly 22%, with sleep bruxism at about 21% and awake bruxism at about 23%, and found North America had the highest sleep-bruxism prevalence of any region at 31%. Missing back teeth accelerate the process, because whatever teeth remain absorb the entire chewing load.

When enough height is lost across enough teeth, the jaws close further than they used to. The chin sits closer to the nose, the lips get less support from behind, and the lower third of the face reads as shorter and more compressed. That is a genuine anatomical change, it is measurable on a clinical examination, and it is the change full-mouth rehabilitation is designed to reverse.

It is also common, and strongly related to age. A systematic review of tooth-wear prevalence by Van 't Spijker and colleagues, [published in the International Journal of Prosthodontics in 2009](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/prevalence-of-tooth-wear-in-adults/), screened 186 references, 13 of which met its inclusion criteria, and found that the predicted proportion of adults with severe tooth wear rises from 3% at age 20 to 17% at age 70, with six of the included studies reporting significantly more wear in men than in women. So the population being marketed to is real: roughly one adult in six reaches their seventies with severe wear. The question is whether you are one of them, which an examination answers and an advertisement cannot.

### What the Research Shows About Restoring It

This is where the evidence gets genuinely useful, and where the marketing pages tend to go quiet. A study published in the Journal of Clinical Medicine in 2021 followed patients whose severely worn dentitions had been rebuilt with direct composite restorations at an increased vertical dimension, and [reported clinical performance out to eleven years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8073648/). In the longest-followed group, five patients with 59 posterior restorations were reviewed at an average of 10.7 years, with a success rate of 78.0% and functional survival of 94.9%. A second group of eight patients with 105 restorations, reviewed at an average of 5.2 years, showed a 78.1% success rate and 100% functional survival.

Two details are worth carrying into any consultation. First, the vertical dimension was increased by 2 to 3 millimeters for every patient — that is the documented scale of the change, and it is small. Second, the gap between "success" and "functional survival" is repairs: ten restorations needed repair in the first group and twenty-three in the second, and all of those were repaired rather than replaced; three others in the first group had to be replaced. Rebuilt teeth are maintained, not installed and forgotten.

## Which Procedures Make Up a Full-Mouth Rejuvenation?

There is no single procedure called a dental facelift. There is a coordinated plan assembled from ordinary restorative and cosmetic dentistry, sequenced so everything meets correctly at the end. Which elements appear depends on what is worn, what is missing and what is failing, and the practice sets out the same building blocks on its [full mouth restoration page](/resources/full-mouth-restoration).

| Element | What it does in the plan | When it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onlays and bonded restorations | Add height back to worn biting surfaces, often additively | Worn but structurally sound back teeth |
| Porcelain crowns | Rebuild a whole tooth where too little sound structure remains | Heavily worn, cracked or previously restored teeth |
| Porcelain veneers | Restore the length, shape and color of front teeth | Visible teeth where the front surface is the problem |
| Dental implants and bridges | Replace missing teeth so the load is shared again | Gaps, failing bridges, teeth beyond saving |
| Orthodontic alignment | Move teeth into positions that let the restorations be conservative | Crowding or bite relationships that would otherwise force heavy cutting |
| [Crown lengthening](/procedures/periodontal-services/crown-lengthening) | Adjust the gum line so tooth proportions read correctly | Short-looking teeth where gum, not tooth, is the issue |

### Additive or Subtractive: The Question Worth Asking

The most useful distinction in this subject is whether height is being added to your teeth or cut into them. Bonded onlays and composite build-ups are largely additive — they place new material on top of worn surfaces, and the study above used exactly that approach. Conventional [crowns](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps) are subtractive: the tooth is reduced so the crown fits over it. Both are legitimate and heavily damaged teeth often need the second, but they commit you to very different amounts of irreversible change, and marketing language about "no drilling" is worth testing against the actual plan.

### Where Veneers Fit, and Their Honest Limits

[Porcelain veneers](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry/porcelain-veneers) handle the visible front teeth well, and they are the most photographed part of a full-mouth plan. The [American Dental Association's MouthHealthy guide](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/veneers) is unusually direct about the trade-off: placing a veneer involves "removing a small amount of enamel from the front and sides" of the tooth, and because enamel is removed, "treatment is not reversible." It also notes that veneers may not suit people who clench or grind their teeth.

That last point deserves a moment, because it cuts against the sales pitch. Grinding is one of the main reasons teeth wear down in the first place, which means a meaningful share of the people being offered full-mouth rejuvenation are in the group the ADA flags as poorer veneer candidates. It does not rule the treatment out. It does mean the plan needs to address the grinding as well as the damage, and a plan that does not mention it at all is incomplete.

Missing teeth change the arithmetic more than anything else, because they remove support the rest of the arch was relying on. [Dental implants](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants) restore that support at the root and fixed bridges restore it across a span, and both need planning before the restorative work begins, since the final bite has to account for them.

## How Is This Different From a Smile Makeover?

The two terms overlap heavily in advertising and mean quite different things clinically. A [smile makeover](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry) is scoped to appearance, usually across the teeth that show when you smile. A full-mouth rehabilitation is scoped to the whole bite, including teeth nobody ever sees, because the bite determines whether the new work survives. A practice using facelift language is almost always describing the second while showing photographs of the first.

| | Smile makeover | Full-mouth rehabilitation | Surgical facelift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What is treated | Visible teeth and gum line | Every tooth, plus the bite relationship | Skin, fat and deeper soft tissue |
| Main goal | Appearance | Function first, appearance with it | Appearance |
| Typical scope | The teeth that show when you smile | Both arches | Face and neck |
| Who provides it | Dentist | Dentist, often with a prosthodontist | Plastic surgeon |
| Changes facial skin | No | No | Yes |

If your concern is limited to how your front teeth look in photographs, a full-mouth plan is more treatment than the problem requires. The [smile analysis questionnaire](/resources/smile-analysis) is a reasonable first pass at working out which category you are in, and our article on [what happens in a smile design consultation](/blog/what-is-smile-design-consultation) covers how that conversation runs.

## What Can Restoring Your Bite Realistically Change About Your Face?

Something real, and less than the photographs imply. Restoring lost tooth height restores the support your lips and the tissue around your mouth were designed to rest on, and it lengthens the distance between nose and chin back toward where it was. People who have genuinely lost several millimeters often notice their lips show more, their mouth corners sit differently, and the lower third of the face reads as less compressed.

### The Change Is Measured in Millimeters

Hold on to the figure from the research: 2 to 3 millimeters. That is a real change to how the lower face is supported, and on a face it is a subtle one. Any presentation implying a dramatic transformation is either showing a case with unusually severe collapse, relying on photographic lighting and angle, or describing something other than what the dental literature reports.

### What Dentistry Does Not Treat

This is the part the term "facelift" quietly borrows and cannot deliver. The [American Society of Plastic Surgeons](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/facelift) describes what a facelift addresses: relaxation and sagging of facial skin, deepening of the folds between the nose and the corners of the mouth, fat that has fallen or disappeared, jowls, and loose skin and excess fat of the neck. Dentistry treats none of those tissues. It works on teeth and on the way the jaws meet, and everything on that list sits outside it.

The same organization is explicit that "a facelift can only be performed surgically" and that "minimally invasive rejuvenation treatments cannot achieve the same results." It also notes that even surgery "does not change your fundamental appearance and cannot stop the aging process." Nothing in restorative dentistry changes the upper or middle thirds of the face — the brow, the eyes, the cheekbones — and nothing in it addresses skin quality. Restoring tooth height does one specific, useful thing, and calling it a facelift oversells it.

## Who Is a Genuine Candidate, and Who Is Not?

The candidacy test is simple to state and requires an examination to answer: has your bite actually lost height, and can that be measured rather than asserted? The signs that warrant a full-mouth conversation are functional ones rather than cosmetic ones.

### Signs Worth Investigating

Front teeth that have visibly shortened or developed flat, even edges are the classic finding, particularly alongside chipping. Several missing or failing back teeth, old crowns and bridges reaching the end of their working life, or difficulty chewing comfortably all point the same way. So does a history of grinding, or being told by a partner that you grind at night.

### Reasons to Be Cautious

If your teeth are essentially unworn and your complaint is about color, small gaps or mild crookedness, there is no lost height to restore, and opening the bite is a much larger intervention than your problem calls for. Whitening, bonding, [clear aligners](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry/invisalign) or a small number of veneers cover that ground with far less irreversible change. Active gum disease or untreated decay also has to be resolved before any of this begins, because restorations placed on an unstable foundation fail early.

The other caution is sequencing. A responsible plan tests a new bite before committing to it, usually with a removable appliance or provisional restorations worn for a period, so that you can find out whether you tolerate the change while it is still reversible. A plan that moves straight to permanent crowns across both arches is skipping the step that protects you.

## What Does Full-Mouth Rejuvenation Cost?

There is no single price, because there is no single procedure. What follows are illustrative market ranges drawn from published guides — not this practice's figures and not an ADA fee schedule. Dental fees have been rising roughly 3 to 4% year over year, about in step with general inflation, according to the ADA Health Policy Institute's *State of the U.S. Dental Economy*. Only an examination produces a number that means anything, and any written plan should itemize what is included.

| Element | Illustrative US market range | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single porcelain crown | $800 — $2,500 per tooth | Varies widely by material |
| Single dental implant, all-in | $4,500 — $7,500 | Post, abutment and crown together |
| Fixed full-arch bridge on 4 — 6 implants | $15,000 — $38,000 per arch | Acrylic hybrid at the low end, zirconia at the high end |

These are published national figures. Bay Area fees run above them; current fees are on the [Crowns](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps) and [Dental Implants](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants) pages.

Two things follow. A full-mouth plan is assembled from individually priced items, so ask for the itemized version rather than a headline number. And a branded program name is a marketing choice, not a different kind of dentistry, so compare the itemized plan rather than the label. For the veneer side of the arithmetic, our [Oakland veneer cost guide](/blog/veneers-cost-oakland-2026) breaks those numbers down separately.

Insurance treats this work by category rather than by name. Restorative items with a clear functional justification — crowns on broken teeth, replacement of failing work — are usually considered under a plan's major services with an annual maximum, while purely cosmetic elements are generally not covered at all. Ask for a written pre-treatment estimate submitted to your plan before work begins.

## How Long Does It Take, and How Long Does It Last?

Timelines run in stages rather than weeks, and a comprehensive plan is normally spread across several months. Diagnosis, records and photographs come first, then any gum or decay treatment, then a trial period in provisional restorations to test the new bite, then the definitive work arch by arch. Cases involving implants add healing time for the bone to integrate, measured in months rather than appointments.

Longevity depends on what was placed and on what caused the wear. The eleven-year study gives the most honest picture available: most restorations were still functioning at a decade, a meaningful minority had needed repair, and the failures were repairable rather than catastrophic. If grinding caused the original damage and is not managed afterwards, the same forces will work on the new restorations — which is why protection at night belongs in a well-built plan and is worth asking about explicitly.

## What Should You Ask Before You Agree to It?

The questions below separate a considered treatment plan from a package. None are confrontational, and any practice comfortable with its own planning will answer them readily.

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Has my vertical dimension actually reduced, and how did you measure it? | Distinguishes a measured finding from an assumption |
| Is this plan additive or subtractive, and how much enamel comes off? | Sets how much of the change is irreversible |
| Will I trial the new bite in provisionals before anything permanent? | The step that lets you change your mind |
| What is causing the wear, and how does the plan address it? | Untreated grinding or erosion will damage the new work too |
| Which teeth genuinely need treatment, and which are elective? | Separates function from cosmetics in the quote |
| What is the maintenance plan, and what typically needs repair first? | Sets a realistic expectation of the years ahead |
| Can I see cases like mine, and speak to a patient who had this done? | Photographs of other people's mouths are not a plan for yours |

If the answer to any of these is a brochure rather than a specific reply about your mouth, that is information worth having. A second opinion before an irreversible full-arch plan is normal, and no reasonable practice objects to one.

Because these plans are assembled from ordinary procedures, the individual guides are often more useful than the umbrella term: [what dental implants really cost](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay) and [veneers or crowns](/blog/veneers-vs-crowns) cover two of the components most often involved.
