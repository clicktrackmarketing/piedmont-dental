# When Should You See a Gum Specialist? 7 Signs It's Beyond a Regular Cleaning

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

You should see a gum specialist — a periodontist — when the inflammation in your gums has stopped responding to routine cleaning and good home care, or when it has started to affect the bone that holds your teeth in place. According to the [American Academy of Periodontology](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/what-is-a-periodontist/), a periodontist is a dentist with three additional years of education beyond dental school in the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of periodontal disease, and in the placement of dental implants. Early gum inflammation is normally managed by a general dentist and hygienist; the referral point sits roughly where the disease has moved from the gum surface into the deeper supporting tissue, or where standard treatment has already been tried and has not held. If you recognize more than one of the seven signs below, the right ask at your next appointment is a full periodontal evaluation, not another routine cleaning.

### Key Takeaways

- A periodontist completes three years of training beyond dental school, specifically in gum disease and dental implants.
- The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that 42% of US adults aged 30 and older have periodontitis, and about 8% have the severe form.
- The AAP describes gum disease as "often silent," with symptoms frequently absent until the advanced stages — which is why measurement matters more than how your mouth feels.
- Repeat deep cleaning of the same sites is a stronger referral signal than any single symptom, because it means the first approach did not hold.
- Smoking, diabetes, genetic susceptibility, grinding and certain medications all lower the point at which specialist assessment becomes sensible.
- Most patients do not need surgery: the AAP notes that many require no further treatment after scaling and root planing, though the majority need ongoing maintenance afterwards.

## Overview

Almost nobody arrives at a gum specialist by deciding to. What usually happens is quieter: you go in for a cleaning, the hygienist mentions that a few readings are deeper than last time, you are booked for a longer appointment, and six months later the same sites are bleeding again. Nothing hurts, so nothing feels urgent, and the question of whether this needs a different level of care never quite gets asked out loud.

This guide is written to help you ask it. It covers what a gum specialist actually treats and how that differs from what your general dentist and hygienist handle, the seven signs that reliably mark the line between a routine cleaning and specialist-level care, the risk factors that move that line earlier, what a proper periodontal evaluation involves, what treatment normally follows and in what order, and what a referral practically means for your time and your records here in the East Bay.

## What Does a Gum Specialist Actually Do?

"Gum specialist" is the plain-English name for a periodontist. The [American Academy of Periodontology](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/what-is-a-periodontist/) defines one as a dentist who specializes in the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of periodontal disease — the chronic inflammatory condition that affects the gums and the bone supporting the teeth — and who also places dental implants. The training is three years on top of a full dental degree, and it is concentrated almost entirely on the tissues around the tooth rather than the tooth itself.

That focus changes what the appointment is about. A general check-up is largely concerned with the teeth: decay, wear, existing restorations, whether anything is cracked. A periodontal assessment is concerned with the foundation those teeth sit in — how deep the space between gum and tooth measures at several points around each tooth, whether it bleeds when measured, whether the bone on an x-ray has changed shape since the last set, and whether any tooth has begun to move.

The AAP notes that patients are typically referred for moderate or severe periodontal disease, for complex implant cases, or when they carry risk factors such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes, respiratory disease, osteoporosis or cancer. That last group matters, because it means the referral decision is not always driven by how bad the gums look on the day. Sometimes it is driven by what else is going on in your health.

### What a Gum Specialist Does Not Handle

It is worth being clear about the boundary in the other direction too, because a lot of patients arrive expecting the wrong appointment. A periodontist does not fill cavities, fit crowns on a decayed tooth, whiten teeth, or straighten them. Those remain the work of a general or cosmetic dentist. The overlap is real — implants, gum contouring for a gummy smile, and crown lengthening before a crown is fitted all sit in both worlds — but the core work is the gum, the bone, and what can be built on them.

## Dentist or Periodontist: Who Handles What?

Most gum problems never need a specialist. Gingivitis, which the AAP calls the mildest form of periodontal disease, is reversible with professional treatment and good home care, and is squarely general-practice work. The division below is the general pattern rather than a rule, because individual cases vary and any dentist may refer earlier if something is not behaving as expected.

| Situation | Usually managed by | What that normally involves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bleeding on brushing, no deep readings | General dentist and hygienist | Routine cleaning, home-care coaching, recheck |
| Gingivitis confirmed at a check-up | General dentist and hygienist | Professional cleaning plus improved daily technique |
| Deeper readings appearing for the first time | General dentist, often with a longer appointment | Full charting, scaling and root planing at affected sites |
| Readings that stay deep after treatment | Referral point | Specialist re-evaluation of the whole approach |
| Bone change visible on x-rays | Periodontist | Detailed assessment, surgical or regenerative options |
| Loose or drifting teeth | Periodontist, promptly | Assessment of remaining support and prognosis |
| Recession that keeps advancing | Periodontist | Cause analysis, grafting where indicated |
| Implant planned where bone is thin | Periodontist or oral surgeon | Grafting, sinus augmentation, staged placement |

## 7 Signs It Is Beyond a Regular Cleaning

These are not seven ways to tell you have gum disease. They are seven points at which routine cleaning has stopped being the right answer, which is a different and more useful question. The table gives the short version; each sign is explained underneath.

| Sign | What it usually points to | What normally comes next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bleeding that returns after treatment | Inflammation that never resolved | Full periodontal charting of every tooth |
| Gums pulling away, teeth looking longer | Recession, with or without bone loss | Measurement, cause analysis, sometimes grafting |
| A tooth that has loosened or drifted | Loss of bone support | Prompt evaluation and a prognosis for that tooth |
| Pus, a recurring abscess, or a returning bad taste | Active infection below the gumline | Prompt assessment and treatment of the site |
| Breath that responds to nothing you try | Bacteria living in deep pockets | Charting rather than another polish |
| Tenderness on chewing in one area | Inflammation reaching the supporting tissue | Assessment of that tooth and the bone around it |
| The same sites deep-cleaned more than once | Treatment that has not held | Specialist review of the whole approach |

### 1. Bleeding That Comes Back After Treatment

Gums that bleed when you brush are common enough that most people learn to ignore them, and on its own bleeding is an early sign rather than an alarming one. What matters is what happens after it has been treated. If you have had a thorough cleaning, improved your home care, and the same sites are bleeding again a few months later, the inflammation was not resolved — it was interrupted. Bleeding that recurs at specific, repeatable locations usually means something is living below the gumline that a brush cannot reach.

### 2. Gums That Have Pulled Away From the Teeth

The AAP lists receding gums among its warning signs, and patients almost always notice it as teeth appearing longer rather than gums appearing shorter. Sensitivity to cold often arrives at the same time, because the root surface underneath has no enamel over it. [Recession](/blog/gum-recession-treatment) has several quite different causes — brushing force, grinding, the position of the tooth in the jaw, and periodontal disease itself — and they lead to different treatments, which is precisely why it is worth having assessed rather than watched. Where the tissue loss is significant, [gum grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/gum-grafting) is the procedure that rebuilds it.

### 3. A Tooth That Has Loosened, Drifted or Changed Your Bite

This one should short-circuit the usual wait-and-see. An adult tooth is held by bone and by the ligament between the two, and it does not become mobile because of anything happening at gum level. If a tooth feels loose, if a gap has opened where there was not one before, or if your bite feels subtly different — teeth meeting in a new order, a partial denture no longer sitting the way it did — the support underneath has changed. The AAP lists loose teeth, changes in bite and altered denture fit together as warning signs for exactly this reason. Book the assessment rather than the cleaning.

### 4. Pus, Recurring Abscesses or a Bad Taste That Returns

Pus between the gums and teeth is on the AAP's warning list, and it is the least ambiguous sign here. So is a swelling on the gum that comes up, discharges, settles, and comes back weeks later. Some people never see either and only notice a salty or metallic taste that keeps returning to the same spot. All three describe an active infection that is being contained rather than cleared, and none of them is a reason to wait for the next scheduled visit.

### 5. Bad Breath That Does Not Respond to Anything

Persistent bad breath appears on the AAP's list of gum disease symptoms, and the distinguishing feature is persistence rather than severity. Breath that improves after brushing, mouthwash or a professional clean is behaving normally. Breath that returns within hours regardless of what you do, particularly alongside any of the other signs here, points at bacteria sitting in spaces that ordinary cleaning does not reach. It is worth mentioning explicitly, because it is the symptom patients are most likely to feel embarrassed about and least likely to raise unprompted.

### 6. Pain or Tenderness When You Chew

Gum disease is not usually painful, which is what makes pain informative when it appears. Tenderness concentrated on one tooth or one area when you bite down suggests that inflammation has reached the tissues taking the load, or that the tooth is moving very slightly under pressure because its support has been reduced. It is easy to attribute to a filling that needs adjusting, and sometimes that is exactly what it is — but it should be measured, not assumed.

### 7. A Cleaning That Has Been Called "Deep" More Than Once

This is the most reliable sign on the list, and the one patients are least likely to recognize as a sign at all. Scaling and root planing — the deep cleaning below the gumline — is the standard first-line treatment for periodontitis, and it works well for a great many people. If the same sites are being treated a second or third time, the first course did not achieve what it was meant to. That is not a reason to lose confidence in the treatment; it is a reason to change the level at which the case is reviewed. The AAP is direct about the limitation: non-surgical procedures "do have limitations," and when they do not achieve optimal periodontal health, gum surgery may be recommended. The practice's [scaling and root planing](/procedures/periodontal-services/periodontal-scaling-root-planing) page sets out what the non-surgical stage involves.

## Why Do These Signs Get Missed for So Long?

Because gum disease does not hurt until late. The AAP puts it plainly: gum disease "is often silent, meaning symptoms may not appear until the advanced stages." Gingivitis, the earliest form, causes gums to redden, swell and bleed easily with "usually little or no discomfort." A condition with no pain and no visible drama in the mirror does not generate appointments, and the years in which it is easiest to treat are frequently the years in which nothing seems to be wrong.

The scale of it is easy to underestimate as well. The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/data-research/facts-stats/fast-facts-gum-disease.html) reports that 42% of US adults aged 30 and older have periodontitis, and that severe periodontitis affects about 8% of adults — figures drawn from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey covering 2009 to 2014 and published by Eke and colleagues in the *Journal of the American Dental Association* in 2018. Note that a number of dental websites still quote an older figure of 47.2% from the earlier survey period; 42% is the current CDC statistic, and the difference is a reminder to check when a number was measured.

The practical consequence is that you cannot self-diagnose your way to the referral point. What tells you where you stand is measurement — the readings around each tooth, compared with last time — which is why regular [dental exams and cleanings](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/dental-exams-cleanings) remain the mechanism that catches this, even when your mouth feels entirely normal.

## Which Risk Factors Lower the Threshold for Referral?

Two people with identical readings do not carry identical risk, and this is where the referral decision often turns. The [AAP's list of gum disease risk factors](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/gum-disease-risk-factors/) is the reference point below. If several of these apply to you, it is reasonable to ask for specialist assessment earlier than the readings alone would suggest.

| Risk factor | Why it moves the threshold, per the AAP |
| --- | --- |
| Tobacco use | "May be one of the most significant risk factors in the development and progression of periodontal disease" |
| Genetic susceptibility | Some people are more likely to develop it "despite diligent at-home oral care habits" |
| Diabetes and related conditions | Studies show connections between periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease, diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis |
| Clenching or grinding | Puts "excess force on the supporting tissues" and may speed the rate at which they are destroyed |
| Certain medications | Oral contraceptives, antidepressants and some heart medicines can affect oral health |
| Stress | Makes it harder for the body to fight off infection, including periodontal disease |
| Age | Older people have the highest rates; over 70% of Americans aged 65 and over have some gum disease |
| Poor nutrition or obesity | Compromises immune response; research suggests obesity may increase risk |

Genetic susceptibility is the one that catches people out most often, because it breaks the assumption that gum disease is a verdict on how well you look after your teeth. It frequently is not. Someone can brush and floss diligently, follow every instruction given, and still lose attachment steadily — and that person needs assessment sooner, not a stricter lecture about home care. If your habits are genuinely good and your readings are still worsening, treat that as information rather than failure, and say so.

## What Happens at a Periodontal Evaluation?

The AAP recommends an annual [comprehensive periodontal evaluation](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/gum-disease-information/comprehensive-periodontal-evaluation/), or CPE, to assess periodontal health and identify conditions that may need additional treatment. It examines six things: your teeth, your plaque, your gums, your bite, your bone structure and your risk factors. That is a broader appointment than most people picture when they hear "gum check", and it is worth requesting by name.

In practice it means every tooth is measured at several points, the readings are written down so they can be compared against previous visits, bleeding points are recorded, tooth mobility is checked, and x-rays are read for changes in the bone rather than only for decay. Your medical history and medications are reviewed as part of the same appointment, because of the risk factors above. Nothing about it is uncomfortable, and it typically takes longer than a standard check-up.

The single most useful thing you can do afterwards is ask for a copy of the chart. Periodontal disease is judged by change over time far more than by any one set of numbers, and a patient holding their own record is much better placed to notice that this year's readings are worse than last year's.

## What Treatment Usually Follows, and in What Order?

Treatment escalates in a fairly predictable order, and knowing the sequence makes it much easier to tell whether what you are being offered is proportionate.

### Non-Surgical Treatment Is the Starting Point

Scaling and root planing comes first in almost every case. The AAP describes it as a deep cleaning of the tooth root surfaces, removing bacterial deposits from below the gumline and smoothing the root so that they are less able to reattach. It is usually done under local anesthetic, often across more than one appointment, and it is genuinely effective. The AAP's own position is encouraging on this point: [many patients do not require additional treatment](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/periodontal-treatments-and-procedures/non-surgical-treatments/) after scaling and root planing.

The honest qualifier that belongs with it is the one in the same AAP sentence: the majority of patients will require ongoing maintenance therapy afterwards to sustain the result. Treatment stabilizes the condition. It does not close the file.

### When Surgery Enters the Conversation

Surgery is what follows when the non-surgical approach has been given a fair run and the readings have not improved. The AAP is explicit that non-surgical procedures have limitations and that gum surgery may be recommended when they do not achieve optimal periodontal health. Depending on what has been lost, that can mean accessing and cleaning deeper areas, reshaping bone, regenerative procedures, [gum grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/gum-grafting) where tissue has receded, [bone grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting) where volume needs rebuilding, or [crown lengthening](/procedures/periodontal-services/crown-lengthening) where a tooth needs more structure exposed before it can be restored.

Some practices also use laser-assisted techniques at one or more of these stages. If that is proposed, the useful questions are what it replaces, what evidence supports it for your specific situation, and how the result will be measured afterwards — the same questions worth asking of any periodontal treatment plan.

## What Happens After Treatment: Periodontal Maintenance

The step patients most often misunderstand is the one after the treatment. A periodontal maintenance appointment is not a rebranded cleaning. It is a longer, more thorough visit that includes re-measuring the sites that were treated, cleaning below the gumline rather than only above it, and comparing this visit's readings with the last set. It is normally scheduled more frequently than a standard six-month recall, because the sites that were once deep are the sites most likely to relapse.

This is where much of the long-term outcome is decided, and also where it quietly falls apart — an interval slips, then slips again, and eighteen months later the readings are back where they started. If you have been treated for periodontitis, the interval you were given is part of the treatment, not a suggestion. The practice's [periodontal maintenance](/procedures/periodontal-services/maintenance) page describes what these visits cover, and [home care technique](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/home-care) does more of the work between them than most people expect.

## Do You Need a Referral to See a Gum Specialist?

No — periodontists accept patients directly as well as by referral, so you can arrange an assessment yourself if you want one. In practice most people do go through their general dentist, and there is a good reason to: your dentist holds the charting, the x-ray history and the treatment record, and a periodontal opinion formed without those is working from a single snapshot rather than a trend.

If you do arrange it yourself, ask your current practice to send your periodontal chart and your most recent x-rays across beforehand. It is a routine request, it avoids repeating a full set of images unnecessarily, and it lets the specialist see whether a reading of a given depth has been stable for three years or arrived in the last six months — which changes the interpretation completely.
