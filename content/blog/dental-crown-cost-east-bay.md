# What a Dental Crown Costs — And Why Two Quotes Can Differ by Hundreds

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A single dental crown in the United States generally falls between $800 and $2,500, and California sits toward the upper end. Published figures disagree openly: national research conducted by ASQ360° puts the average porcelain crown at $1,399, while Delta Dental gives an out-of-network range of $1,100 to $2,000. Three things move your own number most — the material, whether the crown is milled in the office or made in a laboratory, and whether the tooth needs a build-up before a crown can be fitted at all. Two honest quotes can be hundreds apart because they are often quoting different amounts of work. At Piedmont Dental By Design, a porcelain crown on a back tooth is $1,949 to $2,351, and front-tooth crowns start at $2,379; Bay Area fees run above these national figures. Current fees are on the [Crowns (Caps)](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps) page.

### Key Takeaways

- There is no official national crown fee. The American Dental Association discontinued its Survey of Dental Fees in 2023 and says federal law forbids it to set or recommend fees.
- Material is the largest driver of the headline figure, and the cheapest and most expensive options are roughly double apart.
- A core build-up, a post, or a root canal carried out beforehand are separate charges, and they explain most several-hundred-dollar gaps between two plans.
- Same-day milled crowns and laboratory crowns cost broadly the same. The saving is in visits, not dollars.
- Most plans treat a crown as a major service at around 50%, and the annual maximum usually bites before the percentage does.
- Peer-reviewed survival data shows the materials perform far more similarly than their prices suggest.

## Overview

Being quoted for a crown is one of the more uncomfortable moments in dentistry, because the number arrives without much explanation attached to it. You are told a tooth needs a crown, you are given a figure, and you have no way of knowing whether it is reasonable. If you then call another practice and get a different number, the natural conclusion is that somebody is overcharging — and that is usually the wrong conclusion.

This guide takes a crown quote apart so you can read your own. It covers what the published research says and why the sources disagree, what each material costs and how long each lasts in the clinical literature, how same-day crowns compare with laboratory-made ones, the add-ons that appear before the crown itself, how insurance behaves, and the questions that turn two incomparable numbers into a fair comparison.

## What Does a Dental Crown Actually Cost?

The honest answer is a range, and it is wide. A crown is a custom-made cap that covers a damaged tooth, and as the [American Dental Association's MouthHealthy guide](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/crowns) puts it, a crown can "make your tooth stronger and improve its appearance." What it costs depends on how much of the tooth is left, what the cap is made of, and where you live. Two well-known national sources give different answers, and it is worth seeing both rather than a blended figure that hides the disagreement.

| Source | What it reports | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASQ360° national cost research, published by CareCredit | Porcelain crown averages $1,399, range $915 to $3,254 | Research across the 50 states and the District of Columbia, conducted in 2024 |
| [Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/us/en/protect-my-smile/procedures/dental-crowns/treatment-cost.html) | Out-of-network cost for a permanent crown of $1,100 to $2,000 | National dental benefits carrier, published patient guidance |
| Aggregated market guides | Roughly $800 to $2,500 per crown | Compiled published price guides, not a single survey |

### Why Two Published Averages Disagree

Because nobody is measuring the same thing, and no authoritative referee exists any more. The [ADA's Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market) confirms that the Council on Dental Practice "elected to discontinue the Survey of Dental Fees in 2023" after a change in law eliminated safe-harbor disclosure, and states plainly that the ADA "cannot quote fees for dental procedures and is forbidden by federal law to set or recommend fees."

That matters more than it sounds. Every article quoting an "average dental crown fee" is drawing on commercial research, insurer data or aggregated price guides, because no official schedule exists. Treat every published figure as a signpost, not a benchmark.

### Why California Sits Near the Top of Every Range

Cost-of-living differences show up in dental fees the way they show up in rent. Published state-level analyses consistently place California among the most expensive states for restorative work, because overheads here — premises, staffing, laboratory relationships — are higher than in most of the country. The direction of travel is upward too: the same ADA Health Policy Institute page reports consumer dental spending up 4% as of January 2026 against the previous 12 months.

## What Are You Paying For in a Crown Quote?

A crown fee is not a product price. It covers the examination and planning imaging, the appointment in which the tooth is shaped, an impression or digital scan, a temporary crown, the fabrication of the crown, and the appointment in which it is fitted, adjusted and cemented. The ASQ360° research is explicit that its figures "do not include the initial office visit, extraction and postoperative fees," which is one reason the final total so often exceeds the number a patient remembers being quoted.

When a crown is made in a laboratory, part of the fee is a real invoice from a third party, and those charges vary with the technician, the material and how much custom shading a tooth needs — a front tooth matched to its neighbors in three dimensions is a different piece of craft from a molar nobody sees. You can see how the practice describes the procedure on the [crowns and caps page](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps), with examples in the [porcelain crowns gallery](/smile-gallery/porcelain-crowns-caps).

## Does the Crown Material Change the Price?

Yes, and it is the largest single driver of the headline number. The ASQ360° research breaks its national averages out by type, which is the most useful published comparison available because the same methodology was applied to each.

| Crown type | National average | Reported range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Porcelain / all-ceramic | $1,399 | $915 — $3,254 |
| Metallic (including gold alloys) | $1,211 | $821 — $2,861 |
| Porcelain fused to metal, non-gold | $1,114 | $770 — $2,454 |
| Resin or temporary | $697 | $488 — $1,593 |

National price guides broadly agree with those bands while disagreeing at the edges, commonly running porcelain up to about $2,500. Bay Area fees sit above these national averages: at our practice a porcelain crown on a back tooth is $1,949 to $2,351, and front-tooth crowns start at $2,379 because they take more shade-matching and shaping.

### Where Each Material Is Normally Used

Zirconia and modern all-ceramic materials dominate current practice because they are strong enough for back teeth and natural enough for front ones. Porcelain fused to metal, once the default, gives a strong core with a ceramic surface but can show a dark line as the gum recedes. Full-metal and gold crowns remain quietly excellent for molars where nothing shows, being kind to the opposing teeth and very durable, though few patients now want visible metal. Resin and prefabricated crowns are generally provisional.

There is no single best material, only a best material for a particular tooth in a particular bite — which is why a quote given before an examination is not worth much.

## Which Crown Material Lasts Longest?

This is where the price differences start to look less dramatic. A 2026 systematic review in *Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dentistry* pooled [17 studies of tooth-supported fixed restorations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13544153/), with sample sizes from 20 to 21,340 restorations and follow-up from one to 15 years.

| Material | Reported five-year survival | Reported ten-year survival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metal-ceramic | 97.6% — 100% | Up to 100% in the studies reviewed |
| Layered zirconia | 94.0% — 100% | 71% — 91.3% |
| Monolithic zirconia | Within the all-ceramic band | 86% |
| All-ceramic overall | 88% — 100% | Varies by sub-material |

Read the ten-year column rather than the five-year one. At five years almost everything survives, which is why five-year figures are quoted so freely in marketing. The differences appear later, and layered zirconia — a ceramic surface applied over a zirconia core — shows the widest spread, because that surface can chip away from an intact core.

That problem is well documented. A meta-analysis of [CAD/CAM all-ceramic restorations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8161295/) in *Materials* in 2021, covering 18 prospective studies of 669 restorations, found chipping of the veneering ceramic to be the most frequent complication at 44% to 61.4% across follow-up groups. That review examined bridges rather than single crowns, so read it as evidence about the material — and as a large part of why monolithic designs, milled from one solid block, have become the norm.

Two caveats belong with all of this. Survival in a study means the restoration was still in the mouth, not that it still looked new. And no material survives a bite grinding it flat at night.

## Is a Same-Day CEREC Crown Cheaper Than a Lab Crown?

Usually not, and anyone promising a large saving is describing something other than the fee. Same-day crowns are designed and milled in the practice from a solid ceramic block, using CEREC® or a comparable system, which removes the laboratory invoice but adds the cost of the milling unit, the blocks and the training. Published market guides put them at roughly $1,000 to $2,300 — the same band as laboratory crowns — and at our practice a CEREC® crown is priced the same as a lab-made porcelain crown. What you save is time: one appointment instead of two, and no two weeks of eating carefully on a temporary.

The clinical evidence is reassuring on milled ceramic. A six-year study in the *Journal of Advanced Prosthodontics* in 2023 [followed 50 crowns in 25 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9992700/), half lithium disilicate milled on a CEREC unit and half conventional metal-ceramic. The milled crowns showed six-year survival of 96% against 90.8%, and success of 96% against 83.4%. But the authors reported that survival difference was not statistically significant, and those crowns were milled by a technician rather than chairside — so the fair reading is that milled ceramic performs at least as well as the older standard, not that it is proven superior. A separate 15-year follow-up of chairside monolithic lithium disilicate crowns reported survival of 80.1% from a small cohort, worth knowing precisely because it is less flattering than the five-year figures.

Not every tooth is a candidate. Deep margins below the gum, heavily broken-down teeth and certain front-tooth cases still do better with a laboratory technician involved. The practice's [CEREC® page](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry/cerec) explains the process, and there is a fuller walkthrough in the post on [same-day crowns](/blog/cerec-same-day-crowns-oakland).

## What Gets Added Before the Crown Itself?

This is the section most cost guides skip, and it is where the hundreds of dollars between two quotes usually hide. A crown needs something to sit on, so when decay or a fracture has taken most of the tooth away, it has to be rebuilt before it can be prepared — and that rebuilding is a separate procedure with a separate charge.

### Core Build-Up

A core build-up replaces missing tooth structure with a filling material so there is enough shape left to hold a crown, and market guides commonly place it around $150 to $500. It is billed under its own code and is genuinely additional work, not an administrative add-on. It is also the item most often left off a verbal quote, which is why one practice's figure can look lower than another's for identical treatment.

### Post and Core

When almost nothing remains above the gum, a post is seated into the root to anchor the rebuild, typically quoted at around $250 to $650. Whether you need one is a clinical judgment about how much sound tooth is left, not a preference, and it is worth asking about directly because it changes the total noticeably.

### When a Root Canal Comes First

If the nerve inside the tooth is infected or dying, root canal treatment is carried out before the crown is made. It is a separate procedure with its own substantial fee — market guides put it broadly between $700 and $2,100, since molars have more canals than front teeth. So a quote for "a crown" on such a tooth is not the whole number: ask whether the nerve has been assessed, and remember that if both happen in one benefit year they compete for the same annual insurance maximum.

## Why Do Molars Cost More Than Front Teeth?

Partly time, partly access, partly consequence. A back tooth is harder to reach, harder to keep dry, and takes the brunt of your chewing force, which pushes the choice toward stronger and often more expensive materials. Molars also more frequently need the groundwork described above, because they carry the large old fillings that crack under load.

Front teeth carry a different difficulty. The work is less about force and more about appearance: matching translucency, shade and shape so the crown disappears. That is chair time and laboratory craft rather than material cost, and in a visible case it pushes the figure just as high from the other direction.

## What Does Dental Insurance Pay Toward a Crown?

Most plans classify a crown as a major restorative service and reimburse at roughly 50% of the allowed amount after your deductible, typically leaving several hundred dollars to pay. But the percentage is rarely the binding constraint. Three contract clauses do most of the damage, and none appear on a plan's summary page.

### The Annual Maximum Is the Real Ceiling

According to the [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/), about 65% of dental PPOs — the predominant product in the market — carry a maximum annual benefit of $1,500 or more, and between 2014 and 2023 fewer than 5% of enrollees on average reached that maximum. The second figure explains the first: maximums are set at a level most people never approach, which is why they can be set so low, and why a single crown plus a build-up can consume most of a year's benefit.

### The Downgrade Clause

Many plans contain an alternate-benefit provision: if a less expensive material would restore the tooth adequately, the plan pays toward that material and you pay the difference. In practice, an all-ceramic crown on a back tooth may be reimbursed at the rate for a metal-based one. It is not a denial and not an error, and it is the commonest reason a reimbursement comes back lower than expected — so ask your plan administrator whether the clause applies before choosing a material.

### Replacement Limits and Waiting Periods

Plans commonly restrict how often they will pay to replace a crown on the same tooth, so if your existing crown is nearing the end of its life the date matters. Newly purchased individual plans also usually impose a waiting period before major services are covered at all, often several months to a year. A pre-treatment estimate submitted before work begins removes most of this uncertainty — not a guarantee of payment, but the closest thing available, and any practice will submit one on request.

## Why Do Two Quotes for One Crown Differ by Hundreds?

Set aside the possibility that one practice is simply more expensive, and five structural explanations remain, all checkable. The first is material: a quote assuming a metal-based crown and one assuming monolithic zirconia are not quoting the same object. The second is scope — whether a build-up, a post, or root canal treatment sits inside or outside the figure. The third is what surrounds the crown: whether the examination, imaging, temporary and adjustments are billed separately. The fourth is the laboratory, where invoices for the same nominal material genuinely differ. The fifth is how your plan applies its own clauses, which changes what you pay without changing what anyone charges.

### The Codes on Your Treatment Plan

Every procedure is identified by a code from the ADA's Current Dental Terminology set, and reading them is the fastest way to compare two quotes honestly, because the codes are identical between practices even when the fees are not.

| Code | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| D2740 | Crown, porcelain or ceramic, with no metal underneath |
| D2750 | Crown, porcelain fused to a high noble metal substrate |
| D2790 | Crown, full cast high noble metal |
| D2950 | Core build-up, rebuilding the tooth before the crown |
| D2954 | Prefabricated post and core, in addition to the crown |

Ask for an itemized written plan listing the codes. Two quotes carrying the same codes are comparable; two carrying different codes describe different treatment, and the cheaper one may have left something out that you will pay for later.

## How Can You Bring the Cost Down Without Cutting Corners?

Treating the problem early is the largest genuine saving. A tooth that still has enough structure for a [composite filling](/procedures/restoration/composite-fillings) does not need a crown, and one that fractures below the gum while you are deciding may not be restorable at all — at which point the conversation moves to an [extraction and a replacement](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants), costing considerably more than the crown you were avoiding.

Choosing a material appropriate to the tooth rather than to the brochure is the second. A full-metal crown on a molar nobody sees is not a compromise; by the survival data above it is arguably the safest option, and usually cheaper. Timing treatment across two benefit years, where the tooth is stable enough to allow it, can make an annual maximum stretch further, and it is reasonable to ask any practice what payment options it makes available.

What does not work is price-shopping on the headline figure alone. A quote dramatically lower than every other is usually lower for a reason. Compare codes, not numbers.
