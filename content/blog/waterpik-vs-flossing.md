# Waterpik vs. Flossing: Which One Is Better for Your Teeth?

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

For most people with healthy gums, string floss and a Waterpik (water flosser) both work, and the best one is the one you will actually use every day. Floss scrapes sticky plaque off the sides of teeth; a water flosser is easier to use and often better around braces, implants, bridges and sore gums. The research does not crown a clear winner, so your mouth, your dental work and your habits decide.

### Key Takeaways

- The American Dental Association recommends [cleaning between your teeth once a day](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/flossing) with floss or another interdental cleaner, and it lists water flossers among them.
- A 2019 Cochrane review of 35 trials found the evidence for oral irrigators [limited and inconsistent](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968949/), and the evidence for floss low certainty, so neither has proven superiority.
- In one single-use trial, a water flosser plus brushing removed [74.4% of whole-mouth plaque versus 57.7%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24282867/) for string floss plus brushing.
- Floss is cheap and portable; a water flosser costs more up front but is easier for braces, implants, bridges and limited dexterity.
- Using both is fine: flush with water, then floss or use an interdental brush.

## Overview

Brushing twice a day cleans the outer, inner and chewing surfaces of your teeth, but it cannot reach the tight spaces between them. That is where plaque and food collect, and where cavities and gum disease often start. Dental floss and water flossers (often called by the brand name Waterpik) are the two tools people compare most. This guide from our Piedmont office explains how each works, what the research actually shows, who should use which, and how to use them properly.

## Waterpik vs. Floss at a Glance

Here is how the two compare on the things people ask about most.

| | Water flosser (Waterpik) | String floss |
|---|---|---|
| How it cleans | Pulsing water flushes debris and plaque | Scrapes plaque off each tooth side |
| Ease of use | Easier, especially with limited dexterity | Needs practice and good technique |
| Braces, bridges, implants | Reaches around and under them easily | Slow; needs threaders or special floss |
| Sensitive or bleeding gums | Gentle at low settings | Can irritate if used forcefully |
| Cost | Higher up front, plus replacement tips | Low |
| Portability | Needs power and a sink | Fits in a pocket |
| Evidence | Some trials favour it; Cochrane calls the evidence limited | Low-certainty evidence it reduces gingivitis |

## Why Does Cleaning Between Your Teeth Matter?

Cleaning between teeth removes the plaque your toothbrush leaves behind, before it hardens into tartar. The ADA notes that toothbrush bristles alone [cannot clean effectively between these tight spaces](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/floss), and that plaque left at the gumline can lead to bleeding and gingivitis, the early stage of gum disease.

Left alone, that plaque can lead to:

- **Cavities between teeth**, which are hard to see and often found only on X-rays.
- **Gingivitis**, with red, puffy gums that bleed when you brush.
- **Periodontitis**, where gum disease damages the bone that holds teeth in place. You can read how it progresses in our guide to [gum disease stages](https://piedmontdentalbydesign.com/blog/gum-disease-stages).
- **Bad breath**, from bacteria and trapped food.

## What Is a Waterpik?

A Waterpik, or water flosser, is a device that sends a thin, pulsing stream of water between your teeth and along the gumline to flush away food, plaque and bacteria. Most models have a countertop reservoir and a handheld wand; cordless versions are smaller and easier to travel with.

### Pros of Using a Waterpik

- Gentle on sensitive or bleeding gums.
- Easy to use if you have arthritis or limited dexterity.
- Reaches around braces, bridges, implants and crowns where floss is awkward.
- In short-term studies, it reduced gum inflammation and bleeding compared with brushing and floss; see the research section below.
- Many people find it quicker, so they stick with it.

### Cons of Using a Waterpik

- Does not scrape plaque off tooth surfaces the way floss does.
- Costs more up front, and tips need replacing.
- Needs power or charging, and counter space.
- Can be messy while you get used to it.

## What Is Traditional Dental Floss?

Dental floss is a thin strand of nylon or PTFE (Teflon) that you slide between teeth to scrape plaque off each side and from just under the gumline. It comes waxed, unwaxed, as tape for wider gaps and in pre-threaded picks.

### Benefits of Flossing

- Physically scrapes sticky plaque off the sides of teeth.
- Inexpensive and available everywhere.
- Portable; no power needed.
- You can feel when a surface is clean.

### Challenges with Flossing

- Technique matters; snapping floss down or skipping the curve around each tooth leaves plaque behind.
- Hard to use with braces, bridges, implants or limited dexterity.
- Can irritate gums if used too forcefully.
- Many people skip it because it takes effort.

## What Does the Research Actually Say?

The honest answer is that neither tool has proven itself clearly better for everyone. The strongest summary, a 2019 Cochrane review of 35 randomised trials and 3,929 adults, concluded that evidence for oral irrigators is [limited and inconsistent](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968949/). It found very-low-certainty evidence that irrigators may reduce gingivitis at one month, but no clear effect on bleeding sites or plaque compared with brushing alone. For floss, it found low-certainty evidence of less gingivitis than brushing alone.

Individual trials have reported advantages for water flossers, though they are short and small:

| Study | What was compared | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| Goyal et al., 2013 (single use, 70 adults) | Water flosser + manual brush vs string floss + manual brush | [74.4% vs 57.7%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24282867/) reduction in whole-mouth plaque; 81.6% vs 63.4% between teeth |
| Barnes et al., 2005 (28 days, 95 adults completed) | Brush + water jet vs manual brush + floss | Water jet groups had a [significantly greater reduction in bleeding](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16305005/) at 14 and 28 days |
| Worthington et al., 2019 (Cochrane review, 35 trials) | Interdental devices + brushing vs brushing alone | Evidence for irrigators [limited and inconsistent](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968949/); interdental brushes may beat floss for gingivitis |

The same Cochrane review found that interdental brushes may reduce gingivitis more than floss, which is worth knowing if you have wider gaps between your teeth. The practical takeaway: daily cleaning between your teeth matters more than which tool you pick.

## Who Should Use a Waterpik?

A water flosser is often the better choice if you have:

- **Braces or other orthodontic appliances**, where floss is slow and fiddly.
- **Dental implants, bridges or crowns**, where the water reaches under and around the restoration. Our [dental implants](https://piedmontdentalbydesign.com/procedures/restoration/dental-implants) page explains why implant care matters.
- **Gums that bleed or feel sore**, or a history of periodontal treatment; ask us how it fits with your [periodontal maintenance](https://piedmontdentalbydesign.com/procedures/periodontal-services/maintenance).
- **Arthritis, limited dexterity or a tremor**, which make string floss hard.
- **A history of skipping floss**, because a tool you will use beats one you won't.

### Choosing a Water Flosser

If you decide to try one, a countertop model gives steadier pressure and a bigger reservoir, while a cordless model is smaller and easier to travel with. Look for adjustable pressure so you can start gently, and check for the ADA Seal of Acceptance, which a company earns by [submitting evidence of safety and effectiveness](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/floss) to the ADA's scientific council.

## Who Should Stick with Flossing?

Traditional floss works well if you:

- Have healthy gums and tight contacts between teeth.
- Want the lowest-cost option.
- Already floss well and consistently.
- Travel often or prefer a tool that needs no power.

If your gaps are wider, ask us about interdental brushes, which the Cochrane review found may outperform floss for gingivitis.

## Can You Use Both a Waterpik and Floss?

Yes, and many people do. A simple order is to flush with the water flosser first to loosen food and debris, then floss or use an interdental brush to scrape the sides of the teeth, then brush. If that is too much every day, alternate rather than skip, and focus on the areas where your gums bleed.

## How to Use Each One Properly

### How to Floss Correctly

1. Break off about 18 inches of floss and wind most of it around your middle fingers.
2. Hold a short section tightly between your thumbs and forefingers.
3. Slide it gently between two teeth with a back-and-forth motion; don't snap it into the gum.
4. Curve it into a C-shape against one tooth and move it up and down, just under the gumline.
5. Repeat on the neighbouring tooth, then move to a clean section of floss for the next gap.

Our [how to brush and floss](https://piedmontdentalbydesign.com/procedures/cleanings-prevention/how-to-properly-brush-floss) page walks through the same steps with illustrations.

### How to Use a Waterpik

1. Fill the reservoir with lukewarm water.
2. Start on the lowest pressure setting and increase it over a week or two.
3. Lean over the sink, place the tip in your mouth and close your lips enough to stop splashing.
4. Aim the tip at the gumline at about a 90-degree angle.
5. Start with the back teeth and work around, pausing briefly between each tooth.
6. Let the water drain from your mouth into the sink, then empty and dry the reservoir.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Snapping floss down** hard onto the gum, which causes soreness and bleeding.
- **Using the same section of floss** for every gap, which moves plaque around.
- **Running a water flosser too high** before your gums are used to it.
- **Skipping the back teeth**, where plaque and cavities are common.
- **Treating either tool as a replacement for brushing**; both are an extra step, not a substitute.

## Our Take at Piedmont Dental By Design

We recommend whichever tool you will use every day, and we will happily show you both at your next visit. In our view:

- **For most healthy mouths**, string floss done well is still a strong, low-cost default.
- **For implants, bridges and braces**, a water flosser is usually the easier, more thorough choice day to day.
- **For gums that bleed**, cleaning between your teeth more consistently is the first step; persistent bleeding is a reason to have your gums checked, not just to change tools.
- **For wider gaps**, interdental brushes deserve a try.
- **No tool replaces professional cleanings**, which remove the tartar home care can't. See what happens at a [dental exam and cleaning](https://piedmontdentalbydesign.com/procedures/cleanings-prevention/dental-exams-cleanings).

## Frequently Asked Questions

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Is a Waterpik better than flossing?</summary>
<p>Not clearly. A 2019 Cochrane review found the evidence for water flossers limited and inconsistent, and the evidence for floss low certainty. Some trials favour water flossers for bleeding and plaque. For most people, the better tool is the one you will use every day.</p>
</details>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Can a Waterpik replace flossing?</summary>
<p>For many people, yes, as their daily way of cleaning between teeth. The ADA lists water flossers among acceptable interdental cleaners. If you have tight contacts and healthy gums, floss may still remove more sticky plaque from tooth surfaces, so ask us what suits your mouth.</p>
</details>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Should I use a Waterpik before or after brushing?</summary>
<p>Before brushing works well for most people: the water flosser loosens food and plaque between teeth, and brushing then cleans the surfaces with toothpaste. What matters most is doing it every day.</p>
</details>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Is a water flosser good for dental implants and bridges?</summary>
<p>Usually, yes. The water stream reaches under bridges and around implant attachments where floss is awkward. Daily cleaning around implants helps prevent peri-implantitis, an inflammation that can damage the supporting bone.</p>
</details>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>My gums bleed when I floss. Should I stop?</summary>
<p>No. Bleeding usually means the gums are inflamed by plaque, and gentle daily cleaning often reduces it within a couple of weeks. If bleeding continues, book a checkup, because it can be an early sign of gum disease.</p>
</details>

<details class="blog-faq"><summary>Can kids use a Waterpik?</summary>
<p>Older children, especially those with braces, can use a water flosser on a low setting with supervision. For younger children, a parent flossing for them or floss picks are usually simpler. Ask us at your child's next visit.</p>
</details>
