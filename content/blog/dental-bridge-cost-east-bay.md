# What a Dental Bridge Costs in the East Bay

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A conventional three-unit dental bridge, the standard design that replaces a single missing tooth, has a national out-of-network average of about $3,600 according to Delta Dental. At Piedmont Dental By Design a typical three-unit bridge is $5,847 to $6,651; Bay Area fees run above national figures, and current fees are on the [Fixed Bridges](/procedures/restoration/fixed-bridges) page. The part that confuses almost everyone is that a bridge is not priced as one appliance: it is priced per unit, and the crowns on the two anchor teeth each count as a unit alongside the replacement tooth itself. That is why losing one tooth produces a three-unit quote, and only an examination of the anchor teeth produces a real number for your mouth.

### Key Takeaways

- A bridge is billed per unit. The two anchor crowns and the false tooth are three separate line items, not one fee.
- Replacing a single tooth is therefore a three-unit quote, and each additional tooth in the span adds roughly another unit's worth.
- Delta Dental's published national average for a three-unit bridge is about $3,600 out of network. Bay Area fees run higher: at our practice a typical three-unit bridge is $5,847 to $6,651.
- Most plans place bridges in the major tier at 50%, but the annual maximum, not the percentage, is what usually caps what you actually receive.
- A missing tooth clause can exclude a bridge altogether if the tooth was lost before the plan started.
- Material changes both the price and the ten-year survival, and the cheapest material in the published guides is not the one that failed soonest in the research.

## Overview

If you have been quoted for a dental bridge and the number came back higher than you expected, you are almost certainly looking at a per-unit price rather than a per-tooth one. Patients arrive at the practice on Grand Avenue in Piedmont holding a plan that says "three-unit bridge" for one missing tooth, and the first question is nearly always the same: why am I paying for three teeth when I only lost one? The answer is structural rather than commercial. Two of those units are crowns for teeth you still have, because those teeth are what will hold the replacement in place.

This guide explains how a bridge quote is assembled: what counts as a unit, what a bridge typically costs nationally and at our practice, how much of the gap between two quotes comes down to material, what else appears on the treatment plan, what insurance actually contributes, and how the numbers look over ten years against an implant. Every national figure below is a published market range from an insurer or a cost guide, labeled as such; our own fee is stated separately, and none of these replaces an examination.

## Why Is a Bridge Priced Per Unit?

A fixed bridge is one piece of dental work made of several distinct components, and each component is fabricated, fitted and billed separately. The American Dental Association's [MouthHealthy](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/bridges) resource describes a bridge as a fixed partial denture that literally bridges the gap where teeth used to be, attached to the surrounding teeth for support. Those surrounding teeth are the reason the price is not a single number: they have to be reshaped and crowned, and a crown is a substantial piece of laboratory work in its own right.

Every dental claim in the United States is written in the ADA's [Code on Dental Procedures and Nomenclature](https://www.ada.org/publications/cdt), the code set that keeps treatment documented consistently between practices and insurers. A bridge is not one entry in it. The retainer crown on each anchor tooth and the replacement tooth in the middle are reported separately, which is why [Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/us/en/protect-my-smile/procedures/dental-bridges.html) describes the most common bridge as two crowns plus a pontic rather than as a single item. Your quote is the sum of those parts and the laboratory work that joins them.

### What Counts as a Unit

A unit is any tooth position the bridge covers, whether or not a natural tooth is standing there. The pontic — the false tooth that fills the gap — is one unit. The retainer crown on the tooth in front is a second, and the crown on the tooth behind is a third. That is the standard three-unit bridge, and it replaces exactly one missing tooth.

The arithmetic follows from there. Two missing teeth side by side, spanned between the same two anchors, is a four-unit bridge; three missing teeth is five units. Count every position the bridge covers, including the ones that still have roots in them, and you have the number your quote is built from. Delta Dental's [published national average](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/procedures/dental-bridges/treatment-cost/) of roughly $3,600 for a three-unit bridge works out to about $1,200 a unit, and published cost guides commonly put a unit between $700 and $1,500 depending on material and region.

### Why the Anchor Crowns Are Not Free

It is tempting to think of the anchor crowns as packaging around the part you actually need. Each one involves removing enamel from a healthy tooth, taking an impression or digital scan, fitting a temporary, and having a permanent crown made to the same standard as a stand-alone [crown](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps) — the chair time and the laboratory bill are broadly the same either way. That is also the honest argument against judging a bridge on the sticker price alone. If those neighboring teeth already need crowns, the bridge is doing double duty and the value equation improves considerably; if they are pristine, the crowns are a real cost in tooth structure as well as in dollars.

## What Does a Bridge Cost?

The ranges below are published national market ranges drawn from insurer figures and 2026 cost guides. At our practice a typical three-unit bridge is $5,847 to $6,651, and larger bridges cost more; current fees are on the [Fixed Bridges](/procedures/restoration/fixed-bridges) page. No honest quote for your own mouth exists before an examination, a set of [X-rays](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/dental-x-rays) and an assessment of the teeth either side of the gap.

| Bridge type | Units billed | Published range | What moves it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional three-unit (one missing tooth) | 3 | About $3,600 national average (Delta Dental) | Material, condition of the anchor teeth |
| Four-unit (two missing teeth in a row) | 4 | $2,800 – $6,000 | Span length, material, laboratory work |
| Five units or longer | 5+ | Add roughly one unit for each extra tooth | Span length dominates |
| Cantilever (anchored on one side only) | 2 | Below a traditional bridge — two units, not three | Limited to specific positions |
| Maryland / resin-bonded | Pontic plus bonded wings | $1,500 – $2,500 nationally | Front teeth in most cases |
| Implant-supported | Varies with implant count | $4,000 – $16,000 nationally | Implant count, grafting, arch size |

Two things are worth noticing. The traditional and four-unit rows overlap heavily, because material moves the number as much as an extra tooth does. And the Maryland bridge sits well below everything else not as a discount but because it is a different, more conservative restoration, suited to a narrow set of front-tooth cases and not an option for most molar gaps.

### Why Bay Area Quotes Sit Above the National Average

Dental fees track local operating costs closely, and the Bay Area is an expensive place to run a clinical practice. One 2026 published cost guide tiers bridge pricing by setting, putting rural work at roughly $1,800 to $2,800, suburban at $2,200 to $3,500, and major metropolitan areas at $2,800 to $4,200 or more. Our own fee for a typical three-unit bridge, $5,847 to $6,651, sits above all of those national tiers, as Bay Area fees generally do.

Prices are drifting upward across the sector as well. The [ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market) reports that national dental care expenditures reached $189 billion in 2024, up 3.6% on the previous year after adjusting for inflation, and that consumer dental spending was up 4% as of January 2026 against the twelve months before. That is broadly in step with general inflation rather than ahead of it, but it does mean a quote from two years ago is no longer a useful comparison.

## Why Do Two Quotes for the Same Bridge Differ?

Patients often collect two or three estimates and find a spread of a thousand dollars or more for what sounds like the same treatment. Almost always the difference is not margin — it is that the two plans are not describing the same piece of work. Ask for both quotes broken into line items, because a bridge quote that arrives as a single number cannot be checked.

### The Number of Units

This is the most common reason two quotes diverge and the easiest to miss. One dentist may plan a three-unit bridge anchored on the immediate neighbors; another may judge that one of those neighbors cannot carry the load and plan a four-unit bridge taking in the next tooth along. That is a clinical judgment about long-term survival rather than an upsell, but it adds a unit and changes the price accordingly.

### The Condition of the Anchor Teeth

An anchor tooth with a large old filling may need a core build-up before it can hold a crown. One that has cracked may need endodontic treatment first. One that has lost bone support may not be a suitable anchor at all. Each finding is a separate line on the plan and none is optional once identified, which is also why a quote given before X-rays is provisional by definition.

### The Laboratory and the Chair Time

Bridges are made by dental laboratories, and laboratory fees vary with material, with the complexity of the shade match, and with whether the work is milled locally or sent out. Front-tooth work generally costs more than back-tooth work of the same span, because matching a natural adjacent tooth in the smile line takes more shade photography and sometimes a custom-stained result. None of that shows up in the phrase "three-unit bridge".

## Which Material Should You Pay For?

Material is the biggest single lever on the price, and the most expensive option is not automatically the longest-lasting one. A 2026 systematic review in *Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dentistry*, [Influence of Prosthetic Material Type on the Survival of Tooth-Supported Fixed Dental Prostheses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13544153/), gathered the survival data by material. The price ranges beside them come from a separate 2026 cost guide and are national three-unit figures, not our fees.

| Material | Published national range | Survival in the review | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metal-ceramic (porcelain fused to metal) | $2,000 – $2,700 | 100% at ten years in one trial | Strong performer, back teeth especially |
| All-ceramic / lithium disilicate | $2,200 – $3,000 | 84.4% at ten years for monolithic lithium disilicate | Better aesthetics, shorter published survival |
| Zirconia | $2,500 – $3,500 | 75% to 91.3% at ten years, depending on the study | Strength with improved appearance |
| Gold or high-noble alloy | $3,000 and above | Not separately reported in this review | Durable, rarely chosen for visible teeth |

Those survival figures carry a caveat that matters. They are individual studies reported inside one review rather than a single pooled average, and sample sizes and follow-up designs differ between them, so a 100% ten-year figure comes from one trial and not from the whole literature. The pattern is still consistent with what the review found overall: ceramic chipping is the most frequent technical complication and appears more often in layered designs than partially veneered ones, while secondary decay under the crown margins is the leading biological reason bridges are eventually replaced.

The practical reading is straightforward. If the bridge is going somewhere nobody sees, metal-ceramic is both the cheaper option and a well-evidenced one. If it sits in your smile line you are paying for appearance — a legitimate thing to pay for, as long as you know that is what the extra money buys.

## What Else Appears on the Treatment Plan?

The bridge itself is rarely the only line item, and this is the second most common reason a quote surprises people. The additional entries are not padding; each is a separate procedure that has to happen for the bridge to work or to last.

- A comprehensive examination and radiographs, because the bone level around each proposed anchor tooth decides whether the plan is viable at all.
- Extraction of the failing tooth, if it is still in place, billed separately from the bridge that replaces it.
- Ridge preservation or [bone grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting) at the extraction site, which keeps the ridge shape so the pontic does not sit over a visible dip.
- A core build-up on an anchor tooth that has lost too much structure to hold a crown on its own.
- Endodontic treatment on an anchor tooth that turns out to be non-vital, which is its own procedure with its own fee.
- A temporary bridge to wear while the permanent one is made, usually included but worth confirming.
- A night guard where grinding is a factor, since clenching is one of the surer ways to chip ceramic work.

Ask which of these sit inside the quoted number and which are additional. A plan that has already accounted for a build-up looks more expensive on paper than one that has not yet found it is needed.

## How Much Does Insurance Usually Pay?

Dental insurance almost always contributes something toward a bridge, and it almost never covers the amount people expect. The gap between those two facts is explained entirely by the annual maximum. Piedmont Dental By Design keeps its plan information on its [insurance](/resources/insurance) and [financing](/resources/financing) pages, and a pre-treatment estimate can be submitted to your carrier so you see the real number in writing before anything is started.

### The 100-80-50 Structure

Most plans sort procedures into three tiers. [Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/dental-insurance-101/what-does-my-dental-insurance-cover/) sets out the common structure directly: 100% of routine preventive and diagnostic care such as cleanings and exams, 80% of basic procedures such as fillings and extractions, and 50% of major procedures such as crowns, bridges and implants. The [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/) reports the same pattern, with major services covered at the lowest percentage, and notes deductibles typically between $50 and $100, about 46% of them falling between $50 and $99. So a bridge is a major service at 50% in most plans — the number people fix on, and the number that misleads them.

### The Annual Maximum Is the Real Ceiling

The annual maximum is the total your plan will pay across a twelve-month period, whatever the percentages say. NADP reports that about 65% of dental PPOs, the dominant product in the market, carry a maximum annual benefit of $1,500 or more. For most treatment that ceiling is irrelevant — NADP also reports that between 2014 and 2023 fewer than 5% of enrollees reached their annual maximum in an average year. A bridge is one of the few procedures that takes an ordinary patient straight into it. Here is the arithmetic, using Delta Dental's published national average and a common plan design. It is an illustration, not a quote.

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Published national average, three-unit bridge (out of network) | $3,600 |
| Deductible you pay first | $50 |
| Plan share at 50% of the remaining $3,550 | $1,775 |
| Annual maximum available that year | $1,500 |
| What the plan actually pays | $1,500 |
| Your share | $2,100 |

The 50% became roughly 42% the moment the maximum bound. That is the most useful thing to understand before you budget, and it is why the coverage percentage on its own tells you very little.

### The Missing Tooth Clause and Other Fine Print

Three further clauses catch people out, and all three are worth asking about by name. The first is the missing tooth clause: Delta Dental notes that some policies do not cover pre-existing conditions such as missing teeth, so if the tooth was already gone before your plan started, the bridge replacing it may not be covered at all. The second is the waiting period, which NADP confirms applies particularly to major procedures, though the length varies by plan rather than following a standard. The third is a replacement frequency limit — Delta Dental notes plans may require a minimum interval between services such as crowns and bridges on the same tooth, which matters if you are replacing an older bridge rather than having a first one made. None of these is hidden; they are simply written in the plan document rather than the summary page.

## How Does a Bridge Compare to an Implant Over Ten Years?

The full comparison — bone, surgery, the health of the neighboring teeth, and how each option eventually fails — is a subject in its own right, and the short version is that the decision usually turns on the two teeth either side of the gap rather than on price. What follows is only the financial view over a decade.

| Over a ten-year horizon | Traditional three-unit bridge | Single implant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First cost at our practice | $5,847 – $6,651 (typical three-unit) | From $8,474 (post, abutment and crown) |
| Time to completion | Two to three weeks | Several months |
| Insurance treatment | Major service, commonly 50% | Listed as major by some carriers, excluded or reduced by many plans |
| Teeth affected | Two healthy neighbors are crowned | Neighboring teeth left untouched |
| Replacement within ten years | Possible — Delta Dental puts typical bridge life at five to seven years, with over ten not unusual | Less likely |

The last row is what changes the arithmetic. [Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/us/en/protect-my-smile/procedures/dental-bridges.html) puts a typical bridge at five to seven years with proper care, while noting that lasting beyond ten years with good hygiene and regular professional cleanings is not unusual. If a bridge is replaced once inside the decade the up-front saving against an [implant](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants) narrows considerably and can disappear; if it lasts, the bridge stays the cheaper route. Our guide to [what dental implants really cost in the East Bay](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay) breaks down the implant side in full. Where a partial [denture](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) is under consideration instead, the up-front cost is generally lower again, with different trade-offs in comfort and daily maintenance.

## Why There Is No Official National Fee Table Any More

If you have searched for an authoritative national price for a bridge and come up empty, that is not your search technique. The ADA stopped publishing its Survey of Dental Fees in 2023, and the association states that it cannot quote fees for dental procedures and is barred by federal law from setting or recommending them. There is no current official fee table to consult, for a bridge or for anything else.

That absence is why the internet is full of cost pages with confident-looking numbers and no visible source. Every figure in this guide is labeled by origin for exactly that reason — an insurer's published average, a trade body's stated plan structure, a named systematic review, or a published cost guide. When a number in a cost article carries no source at all, treat it as an estimate rather than a benchmark.

## How Do You Bring the Number Down Without Cutting Corners?

There is a difference between paying less and buying less, and the useful moves are the ones that do not shorten the life of the work. Ask for the plan broken into line items and check the unit count first, because a four-unit plan and a three-unit plan are genuinely different treatments and the extra unit should have a clinical reason you can hear explained. Ask what the material is doing for you in that specific position, since metal-ceramic on a molar is a well-evidenced choice rather than a compromise. Ask what happens if an anchor tooth fails later, and whether the bridge would then need replacing entirely.

Timing is the other lever, and the one most people never use. Because the annual maximum resets, treatment that can safely be staged across two benefit years may draw two maximums instead of one — the extraction and any grafting in the first year, the bridge in the next. That is only appropriate where the delay does not compromise the outcome, so it is a clinical conversation rather than a billing trick, but it is worth raising. And where the neighboring teeth are healthy and untouched, have the implant conversation properly before committing to crowning them, because that decision cannot be reversed later.

## Get a Real Number Before You Decide

A published range is a starting point and nothing more. The figure that matters to you depends on how many units your gap actually needs, what condition the anchor teeth are in, which material suits the position, and what your plan will contribute once the annual maximum is taken into account. All four are answerable in a single visit, and a written pre-treatment estimate submitted to your insurer turns an estimate into a number.
