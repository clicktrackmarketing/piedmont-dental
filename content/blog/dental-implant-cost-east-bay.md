# What Dental Implants Really Cost in the East Bay (2026 Breakdown)

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A single dental implant in the East Bay typically runs between $4,500 and $7,500 all-in for the three parts you are actually paying for: the implant post, the abutment, and the crown. Published guides disagree at the top end — one Berkeley guide caps it at $6,500, a California-wide guide puts the Bay Area at $7,500 — and that spread is itself worth knowing before you compare quotes. Bay Area pricing generally sits above the national average, and the largest single variable is whether you need [bone grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting) first. No honest quote exists before an examination and a 3D scan, because the bone is what decides the plan. Bay Area fees run above these national figures. Current fees are on the [Dental Implants page](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants).

### Key Takeaways

- An implant is priced as three separate components, not one flat fee, which is why quotes are difficult to compare side by side.
- Bone grafting is the most common reason two quotes for "one implant" differ by thousands of dollars.
- Alveolar ridge width can shrink by up to 50% in the first year after a tooth is lost, so waiting can add a grafting cost that did not previously exist.
- Full-arch (All-on-4) work is priced per arch, and the prosthetic material moves that number more than the number of implants does.
- Most dental plans treat implants as a major service with an annual maximum that will not cover the whole treatment.
- Smoking is the single largest modifiable risk to implant survival documented in the research.

## Overview

If you have been quoted a price for a dental implant and it did not match what you read online, you are not being misled. You are comparing a bundle to a component. The word "implant" is used loosely to mean three different things that get billed separately, and whether a fourth item appears on your treatment plan depends entirely on what your jaw looks like on a scan.

This guide breaks the number apart so you can read your own quote properly, understand what moves it up or down, and know which questions to ask before you agree to anything. It covers the itemized components, the five factors that drive price variation, how insurance actually behaves, the treatment timeline, and what the research says about how long implants last.

## What Are You Actually Paying For in a Dental Implant?

An implant is three separate manufactured components, plus the surgical and restorative chair time to place and fit them. Understanding the split is what allows you to compare two quotes honestly, because a practice quoting only the post is not quoting the same thing as a practice quoting the finished tooth.

| Component | What It Does | Typical East Bay Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Implant post (fixture) | Titanium screw placed in the jawbone, acting as the replacement root | $1,800 — $2,500 |
| Abutment | Connector that screws into the post and carries the visible tooth | $400 — $700 |
| Crown | The porcelain tooth you see and chew with | $1,500 — $2,500 |
| **Complete single implant** | **All three, straightforward case** | **$3,700 — $5,700** |

Those component ranges sum to roughly $3,700 to $5,700 for the parts alone. Published guides then quote a higher all-in figure because a real case includes chair time, imaging and follow-up: one Berkeley guide puts a complete single implant at $4,500 to $6,500, while a California-wide guide puts the Bay Area specifically at $5,000 to $7,500. Both are honest, and the gap between them is exactly why an itemized written plan matters more than a headline number. The only figure that means anything is the one produced after an examination. The [American Dental Association's MouthHealthy guide](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/implants) describes implants as being "made up of titanium and other materials that are compatible with the human body," and you can see how the practice describes the procedure itself on the [dental implants page](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants), along with current fees, which sit above the national figures in this table.

One practical consequence is worth carrying into any consultation. When a headline price looks dramatically lower than the range above, check which of the three components it actually covers. A quote for "an implant" that includes only the post is not comparable to a quote that includes the finished crown, and the gap between them is usually most of the cost.

## Why Does the Price of a Dental Implant Vary So Much?

Five factors do almost all of the work. Bone grafting is the largest and has its own section below; the other four explain most of the remaining spread between quotes.

### 1. Implant Brand and Crown Material

Established implant systems with long published track records cost more than newer or economy systems, and that premium buys documented performance rather than branding. On the restoration side, zirconia and higher-grade porcelain crowns cost more than basic ceramic. Both choices are worth asking about by name, because they are the parts of the quote a practice can vary without you noticing.

### 2. Where the Implant Sits in Your Mouth

A front tooth is an aesthetic problem as well as a structural one. Matching the shade, shape and gum contour of a visible tooth so that it disappears into your smile takes considerably more planning and chair time than a molar that nobody sees. Cases in the smile zone therefore sit toward the upper end of the range.

### 3. How Many Implants You Are Placing

Per-tooth cost typically falls when several implants are placed during one surgical visit, because the fixed costs of the procedure are shared across them. Multiple adjacent implants often land in the $3,500 to $5,500 per-tooth range rather than the single-tooth range, which is why replacing three teeth rarely costs three times as much as replacing one.

### 4. Complexity and Number of Appointments

Extraction first, a healing period, a temporary tooth, or a sinus lift near the upper molars each add a stage to treatment, and each stage carries its own cost. This is also where timelines stretch, so it is worth asking early how many appointments your specific case requires.

## What Do Full-Arch and All-on-4 Implants Cost?

Replacing every tooth in an arch is priced completely differently from replacing one, because you are no longer buying one implant per tooth. Four to six implants carry a single fixed bridge, which is why the per-tooth economics improve dramatically even though the headline number is larger.

| Option | What It Is | Typical Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiple adjacent implants | Individual implants placed in one surgical visit | $3,500 — $5,500 per tooth |
| Implant-supported denture (overdenture) | A denture that clips onto 2 — 4 implants, still removable | $6,000 — $15,000 per arch |
| All-on-4 / All-on-6, acrylic hybrid | Fixed full-arch bridge on 4 — 6 implants | $15,000 — $25,000 per arch |
| All-on-4 / All-on-6, zirconia | Same, in a harder and more lifelike material | $25,000 — $38,000 per arch |
| Full mouth, both arches | Two arches restored | $35,000 — $70,000 |
| Traditional full-mouth implants | 16 — 20 individual implants | $60,000 — $90,000+ |

### Why the Material Changes the Price So Much

The single largest variable in a full-arch quote is not the number of implants. It is what the teeth are made of. An acrylic or PMMA hybrid arch is lighter and cheaper, and it wears and stains over time, so it is generally expected to be replaced or refurbished. A zirconia arch costs substantially more, resists wear and staining far better, and is intended to last considerably longer.

Two quotes that both say "All-on-4" can therefore be ten thousand dollars apart and both be honest. Ask which material is included before comparing anything, because the number is close to meaningless without it.

### What the Per-Arch Price Usually Excludes

Full-arch quotes are frequently given as a clean per-arch figure that assumes a straightforward mouth. Extractions of any remaining teeth, bone grafting, and 3D imaging often sit outside it and can add several thousand dollars. When you ask for a written plan, ask specifically whether extractions and grafting are inside or outside the quoted number.

## What Else Appears on an Implant Treatment Plan?

The three components are the core of the cost, but they are rarely the whole of it. These items appear routinely and are worth anticipating rather than being surprised by.

| Line Item | When It Applies | Typical Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consultation and 3D imaging | Almost always, for planning | $200 — $400 (sometimes included) |
| Simple extraction | The tooth is still in place | $150 — $300 |
| Surgical extraction | Broken, impacted or root-fractured tooth | $250 — $600 |
| Bone grafting | Insufficient bone volume at the site | $500 — $3,000 per site |
| Sinus lift | Upper back teeth, insufficient vertical bone | $1,500 — $5,000 |
| Temporary tooth | Visible gap during healing | $300 — $800 |

None of these are upsells by definition. Each exists because of something specific about your mouth, and a good treatment plan will say plainly which apply to you and why. What you should not accept is a plan that leaves them unpriced until after treatment has started.

## Why Does Bone Grafting Change the Number So Much?

Because when a tooth is lost, the bone that held it begins to disappear, and it happens faster than most people expect.

Tan and colleagues' systematic review of post-extraction dimensional changes in humans, published in *Clinical Oral Implants Research* in 2012 and [reproduced in a 2026 review in *Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dentistry*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13018099/), found horizontal bone loss in the range of 29% to 63% and vertical loss of 11% to 22% within six months of extraction. Ridge width can reduce by up to 50% in the first year, with roughly two-thirds of that loss occurring inside the first three months.

That matters clinically and commercially. An implant needs sufficient bone to sit in, and if the bone has already gone it must be rebuilt first. [Bone grafting](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting) is a separate procedure with its own fee, its own healing period and its own follow-up scan. A small graft placed at the same time as an extraction is a modest addition to a treatment plan. A larger ridge augmentation or a sinus lift, undertaken months or years after a tooth was lost, is a significantly bigger one.

This is the single most useful thing to understand about implant pricing: the cost tends to rise the longer you wait, because the procedure you eventually need is not the procedure you would have needed at the time. If you are weighing an extraction now and an implant at some point later, it is worth asking at that first appointment whether a graft placed alongside the extraction would preserve your options. It frequently does. If you are comparing an implant against a removable alternative instead, the [dentures and partial dentures page](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) sets out how that option works.

## Does Dental Insurance Cover Implants?

Partially at best, and the annual ceiling is usually a bigger problem than the coverage percentage.

Most dental plans classify implants as a major restorative service, which typically means reimbursement at a lower percentage than fillings or cleanings, often after a waiting period. The harder limit is the annual maximum, and for implant patients it bites in a way it does not for anyone else.

According to the [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/), about 65% of dental PPOs carry an annual maximum of $1,500 or more, and between 2014 and 2023 fewer than 5% of enrollees on average ever reached their cap. That second figure is the important one. Annual maximums are set at a level most people never touch, which is precisely why they are set so low, and why a single implant can consume an entire year's benefit on its own.

### Does a DHMO or a PPO Cover Implants Better?

This is the question almost nobody asks, and for a large case it can matter more than the coverage percentage.

| | DPPO | DHMO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual maximum | Usually applies, commonly $1,500+ | Typically none |
| Deductible | Usually applies | Typically none |
| Out-of-network care | Partially reimbursed on many plans | Generally not covered |
| Major services | Percentage share, capped by the maximum | Often a flat fee per procedure |

[Delta Dental](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/dental-insurance-101/dental-hmo-vs-ppo-dental-insurance-what-is-the-difference/) sets out the structural difference plainly: a DHMO typically carries no annual maximum and no deductible, but it will not cover care outside its network. For a patient facing a large multi-implant or full-arch case, the absence of an annual cap can outweigh the loss of choice. For a single implant with a dentist you already trust, it usually does not.

### How to Get the Most Out of the Benefit You Have

- **Request a pre-treatment estimate.** Your dental office submits the proposed treatment to your insurer, and the insurer responds with what it will actually pay. It is not a binding guarantee, but it removes most of the guesswork from your planning.
- **Ask whether staging across two benefit years makes clinical sense.** The surgical and restorative phases often fall in different years naturally, which can put two annual maximums against one treatment. Sometimes forcing that split produces worse dentistry, so the answer you get should be clinical rather than financial.
- **Ask which parts of the plan are covered separately.** Extractions, imaging and sometimes bone grafting can be covered even when the implant itself is not, because they are billed under different codes.
- **Check whether your plan has a waiting period for major services**, particularly if you have recently changed employers or policies.

Dental costs generally have been drifting upward. The [American Dental Association's Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market) puts national dental expenditure at $189 billion in 2024, up 3.6% on 2023 after adjusting for inflation, with consumer dental spending up 4% in the twelve months to January 2026. Any quote more than a year old is worth refreshing before you budget against it.

## How Long Does the Whole Implant Process Take?

Longer than most people expect, and the waiting period is doing something important.

A straightforward case with good bone typically runs three to six months from placement to final crown. That gap is osseointegration, the process by which bone grows onto and fuses with the surface of the titanium post. It cannot be rushed, and it is the reason implants perform as well as they do over decades.

| Stage | What Happens | Typical Duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consultation and 3D scan | Planning; grafting identified or ruled out | 1 appointment |
| Extraction and/or grafting, if needed | Site prepared and allowed to heal | 3 — 6 months |
| Implant placement | Post placed, usually under local anesthetic | 1 appointment |
| Osseointegration | Bone fuses to the implant surface | 3 — 6 months |
| Abutment and crown | Digital scan or impressions, then fitting | 1 — 2 appointments |

If a practice offers you a materially shorter timeline, ask what specifically about your case allows it. Immediate-placement protocols are genuine and appropriate in selected cases, but they are not appropriate in all of them, and the answer should be about your bone rather than their schedule.

## How Reliable Are Dental Implants, Actually?

Very reliable, and the research on this point is unusually good.

A [large-scale electronic dental registry study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11856851/) published in the *Journal of Functional Biomaterials* in 2025 analyzed 158,824 implants placed in 53,874 patients. It reported overall implant survival of 97.79%, and an early failure rate during the osseointegration phase, meaning before the crown was ever fitted, of just 1.56%. Real cases the practice has treated are shown in the [implant smile gallery](/smile-gallery/dental-implants).

The risk factors matter because one of them is entirely within your control. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in [*Medicina*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8780868/), pooling 292 publications and more than 150,000 implants, found smokers had roughly 2.4 times the odds of implant failure (OR 2.402) and greater marginal bone loss than non-smokers. Smoking remains the largest modifiable threat to a placed implant, and that figure comes from the pooled evidence rather than a single cohort.

## Implants or Dentures Over Ten Years?

A single-visit price comparison flatters dentures and a lifetime comparison flatters implants, so the honest framing is somewhere in between: what does each option actually cost you across a decade, including the things nobody quotes for.

| | Implant | Conventional Denture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Up-front cost | Higher | Lower |
| Expected replacement | Not routinely replaced | Relines and remakes over time |
| Effect on the jawbone | Loads the bone, helping preserve it | Does not load the bone; ridge continues to resorb |
| Daily upkeep | Brushed like a natural tooth | Removed, cleaned, adhesive for some wearers |
| Ongoing fit | Stable | Changes as the ridge changes |

The bone point is the one that compounds. Because a denture rests on the ridge rather than loading it, the resorption that begins at extraction carries on underneath, which is why dentures that fitted well eventually stop fitting well and need relining or remaking. Each of those is a cost, and the same shrinkage progressively narrows the option of placing implants later.

None of this makes dentures the wrong answer. They remain the right answer for plenty of people, particularly where bone volume, general health or budget rule out surgery. It does mean that comparing only the first invoice will mislead you. If you are weighing the two, the [dentures and partial dentures page](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) sets out how the removable option works in practice.

## What Should You Ask Before You Accept an Implant Quote?

Most cost surprises trace back to a question nobody asked. Take these six to your consultation and write the answers down, because they are what make two quotes genuinely comparable.

1. Does this quote include the post, the abutment and the crown, and if not, what is missing?
2. Do I need bone grafting, and is that cost inside this figure or additional to it?
3. Which implant system are you using, and how long has it been on the market?
4. Will I have a temporary tooth during healing, and is it included in the price?
5. What happens, clinically and financially, if the implant fails to integrate?
6. Can you submit a pre-treatment estimate to my insurer before I commit?

A practice that answers all six plainly is giving you what you need to compare quotes honestly. For a wider look at implants as a treatment rather than a price, see [Dental Implants in Piedmont, CA](/blog/dental-implants-piedmont-ca).
