# Gum Recession: Why It Happens and Every Treatment That Actually Works

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

Gum tissue that has receded does not grow back on its own, and no toothpaste, rinse, oil or supplement has been shown to bring it back. What treatment can do is stop the recession getting worse and, where it matters, cover the exposed root surgically. The right treatment depends entirely on the cause: gum disease is treated by cleaning below the gumline, brushing damage is treated by changing how you brush, and lost coverage is restored with a gum graft, which in pooled trial data covers around 83% of the exposed root on average.

### Key Takeaways

- Receded gum tissue does not regenerate by itself. Surgery is the only way to add tissue back.
- Recession has several distinct causes, and the treatment that works depends on which one you have.
- Gum disease and brushing damage are the two most common causes, and they call for opposite fixes.
- Brushing frequency is not the risk factor. Force, technique and bristle hardness are.
- Grafting is well studied: pooled results show about 83% mean root coverage, with complete coverage in roughly six cases out of ten.
- Recession that is mild, stable and not sensitive may need nothing more than accurate measurement and monitoring.

## Overview

Most people find their own gum recession by accident. A tooth looks longer than it used to in the bathroom mirror, cold water hits one spot and makes you wince, or your tongue catches a small ledge where the enamel ends and the softer root surface begins. Then you search for it, and the internet tells you two contradictory things at once: that receding gums are serious and permanent, and that a particular oil, powder or rinse will grow them back. Both cannot be true, and it is the second one that is wrong.

This guide covers what gum recession is, the specific causes and how to work out which applies to you, what the evidence says about regrowth, the treatments that do not work and are still widely sold, and the real ladder of treatments that do — from measuring and monitoring, through desensitising and bonding, to gum grafting, including how well grafting performs in published trials.

## What Is Gum Recession, and How Common Is It?

Gum recession is the migration of the gum margin away from the crown of the tooth, exposing part of the root that is normally covered. The root is not enamel. It is a softer, more porous surface with microscopic tubules running to the nerve, which is why an exposed root is often sensitive and why it wears and decays more readily than the crown above it.

It is far more common than most people expect, and it accumulates with age. United States national survey data, summarised in a review published in *Case Reports in Dentistry*, found that [88% of adults aged 65 and over and 50% of adults aged 18 to 64 had recession at one or more sites](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3467775/). The same review shows how sharply it climbs: at ages 30 to 39, prevalence was 37.8% and affected 8.6% of teeth on average, while by ages 80 to 90 prevalence reached 90.4% and the average extent had risen to 56.3% of teeth. It is not only an older person's problem either — a study of 1,430 Greek adults aged 18 to 38 found an [overall prevalence of 63.9% in that young adult group](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4134852/).

So what matters clinically is not whether you have any recession, but how much, where, whether it is progressing, and what is driving it. Recession is measured in millimeters from a fixed landmark on the tooth and recorded site by site, and the number that counts is the change between one visit and the next. A single measurement tells you where you are. Two measurements a year apart tell you whether you have a problem.

## Why Do Gums Recede?

Recession is not one condition with one cause. It is an end point that several different processes arrive at, and the useful question is which process is at work in your mouth. The recognized contributing factors include plaque and calculus, aggressive toothbrushing, a high frenal attachment, tooth position in the arch, orthodontic tooth movement, poorly designed partial dentures, smoking, and restoration margins that sit below the gumline — a list set out in the same *Case Reports in Dentistry* review.

### Periodontal Disease

The most consequential cause is gum disease, because it destroys the bone underneath the gum rather than just the gum itself. Where supporting bone is lost, the gum has nothing to sit on and follows it down. This is the version of recession that also loosens teeth, which is why recession is never a purely cosmetic finding until someone has ruled the disease out.

It is common enough that this should be the first thing checked. The [CDC reports that 42% of adults aged 30 and over have periodontitis, and about 8% have severe periodontitis](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/data-research/facts-stats/fast-facts-gum-disease.html), drawing on national survey data collected between 2009 and 2014. Inflammation is also the strongest measurable risk indicator for recession itself: in the Greek adult study above, gingival inflammation carried an odds ratio of 4.54, ahead of every other factor measured. The practice's page on [periodontal gum disease](/procedures/periodontal-services/what-is-periodontal-gum-disease) sets out how the disease progresses and where it stops being reversible.

### Brushing Force, Not Brushing Frequency

Aggressive brushing is the cause most articles name first. It is real, but it is described so imprecisely that it tends to make people brush less rather than better. The distinction that matters is between how often you brush and how hard.

Frequency is not the risk factor. In the Greek study, frequent toothbrushing carried an odds ratio of 0.98 — effectively no association — and the authors noted a weak negative relationship between brushing frequency and recession. The wider evidence is genuinely mixed: they cite the systematic review by Rajapakse and colleagues, published in the *Journal of Clinical Periodontology* in 2007, which found that of 17 studies examined, eight reported a positive association while two found none.

What is not in doubt is the instrument and the pressure. The [American Dental Association recommends a soft-bristled toothbrush specifically because soft bristles minimize the risk of gingival abrasion](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/toothbrushes), and advises gentle pressure for the same reason, noting that medium bristles remove plaque effectively but carry more risk to the tissue. So brushing twice a day is not damaging your gums, and stopping will make things worse. Scrubbing hard, sawing horizontally and using a firm brush plausibly are. If nobody has ever watched you brush, the practice's guide to [proper brushing and flossing technique](/procedures/cleanings-prevention/how-to-properly-brush-floss) is a reasonable place to check yourself against.

### Grinding and Clenching

Grinding and clenching load the necks of teeth in a way ordinary chewing does not. Occlusal trauma and parafunctional habits sit in the literature as aggravating factors for recession, and they tend to travel with wedge-shaped notches at the gumline called non-carious cervical lesions. Where recession and those notches appear together on the same teeth, the mechanical load is worth investigating rather than assuming the toothbrush did all of it. Treating the recession without addressing the grinding treats the symptom — and that stays true after surgery, since tissue grafted over a root that is still being overloaded has to survive the conditions that contributed to the problem.

### Anatomy, Smoking and Everything Else

Some people are more susceptible for reasons entirely outside their control. A thin gum phenotype covers the root with less tissue to begin with and is more vulnerable to both inflammation and mechanical trauma. Teeth sitting prominently in the arch, close to the outer edge of the bone, have thinner bone and thinner gum over them from the start, and a high frenal attachment — the small band of tissue between lip and gum — can pull at the margin. If you brush gently, floss, do not smoke and still have recession on your canines or lower front teeth, anatomy is the likeliest explanation, and the answer is not to try harder at home care that is already adequate.

Smoking is an established risk factor: in the Greek adult study, cigarette smoking carried an odds ratio of 1.97, roughly doubling the odds after adjustment. It also worsens the underlying gum disease and reduces how well surgical sites heal, which makes it relevant twice over. Orthodontic movement can carry teeth beyond the envelope of bone that supports them, poorly designed partial dentures can rest on and irritate the gum margin, and restoration margins placed below the gumline can keep the tissue inflamed. None of these are reasons to avoid orthodontics, dentures or restorations, but they are reasons to have recession checked at each review rather than only when it starts to hurt.

## Can Receding Gums Grow Back on Their Own?

No. This is the single most important thing to be clear about, and it is where a great deal of money changes hands unnecessarily.

Once the gum margin has migrated down the root, it does not migrate back up. There is no rinse, oil, gel, supplement, diet or brushing method shown to return a receded margin to its original position. What can improve — sometimes dramatically — is inflammation. Swollen, red, bleeding tissue that settles down after a good cleaning and better home care will look and feel considerably better, and the margin may even appear to sit slightly differently as the swelling resolves. That is genuine improvement and worth having. It is not regrowth.

The distinction explains almost every "I healed my receding gums" account online. Those are, in the overwhelming majority, descriptions of inflammation resolving. The tissue is healthier. The root is still exposed. Adding tissue back requires surgery, which is what a gum graft is for, and it is the only intervention with controlled trial evidence behind it for covering an exposed root.

## What Does Not Work for Receding Gums?

Knowing what to stop paying for is as useful as knowing what to start. Most of what is sold for receding gums either addresses inflammation, which is worth doing but is not the same as coverage, or addresses nothing at all. Two habits deserve naming separately, because they are intuitive and actively counterproductive: brushing harder to clean the area better adds mechanical trauma to a site that may already be suffering from it, and brushing less often, out of fear of causing damage, lets plaque accumulate at the exact margin where inflammation does the most harm.

| Commonly promoted | What the evidence supports | What it will not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oil pulling | Some interest in bacterial load; no root-coverage evidence | Restore a receded gum margin |
| "Gum regrowth" drops and serums | Nothing; the claim runs against the biology | Regenerate lost tissue |
| Herbal and saltwater rinses | May reduce inflammation modestly | Cover an exposed root |
| Brushing harder | Nothing; adds mechanical trauma | Reverse or slow recession |
| Desensitising toothpaste | Good evidence for reducing sensitivity | Change the position of the gum |
| Gum grafting | Controlled trial evidence for root coverage | Work indefinitely if the cause remains |

## What Are the Real Treatment Options, From Least to Most Involved?

Recession treatment is a ladder, not a single procedure, and most people never need to climb all of it. The rung you belong on is set by three things: whether the recession is progressing, whether the exposed root is symptomatic, and whether it matters to you aesthetically.

| Rung | When it applies | What it does | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measure and monitor | Mild, stable, not sensitive, cause identified and removed | Confirms it is not progressing | Cover the root |
| Treat the gum disease | Inflammation, bleeding, calculus below the gumline | Stops the destructive process driving recession | Restore lost tissue |
| Change the mechanical cause | Brushing trauma, grinding, appliance or margin irritation | Removes the force that caused it | Reverse existing recession |
| Desensitise | Exposed root that hurts to cold, air or brushing | Reduces or removes the symptom | Change how the tooth looks |
| Bond the exposed root | Sensitivity plus a visible or notched root surface | Covers and protects the root with composite | Restore gum tissue |
| Gum graft | Progressive, sensitive, aesthetically significant, or thin tissue at risk | Adds tissue and covers the root | Guarantee complete coverage |

The first rung deserves more respect than it usually gets. Recession that has stopped moving, does not hurt and does not show when you smile is a legitimate candidate for accurate measurement and review rather than intervention. The comparison over time is the treatment. Committing to surgery for a stable two-millimeter recession on a back tooth is not automatically the conservative choice.

### Treating the Underlying Gum Disease

Where inflammation and calculus below the gumline are present, this comes before everything else. Cleaning the root surfaces below the gum — scaling and root planing, often called a deep cleaning — removes the deposits that keep the tissue inflamed. It is not a cosmetic procedure and it does not put tissue back, but it stops the process that is taking tissue away. Grafting over active disease is building on a moving foundation: the sequence is disease first, stability second, coverage third. The practice's page on [scaling and root planing](/procedures/periodontal-services/periodontal-scaling-root-planing) describes the appointment, and [periodontal treatment](/procedures/periodontal-services/treatment) covers the wider picture.

### Managing the Sensitivity

For many people the exposed root is not a cosmetic issue at all. It simply hurts, and removing the pain resolves the complaint. The usual first step is a desensitising toothpaste used consistently — the over-the-counter products are generally built around potassium salts or stannous fluoride — followed by in-practice application of a desensitising agent if that is not enough.

The comparative evidence is better than most people assume. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *Journal of Oral Biology and Craniofacial Research* pooled [31 randomised controlled trials covering 2,721 participants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13540673/) and found a significant overall reduction in dentine hypersensitivity, with a pooled standardised mean difference of −1.02. Laser therapy showed the largest effect at −2.11, with bioactive glass at −1.08 and arginine–calcium carbonate at −1.04 also reaching significance, while oxalate-based agents and resin and varnish systems both produced confidence intervals crossing zero. The authors flagged high heterogeneity and ran no formal certainty assessment, so treat these as useful comparisons rather than a settled ranking.

### Bonding to Cover the Root

Where the exposed root is notched, visibly darker than the crown, or persistently sensitive, tooth-colored composite can be bonded directly onto the root surface. It seals the exposed dentine, restores the contour and makes the transition far less noticeable. It is quicker than surgery, needs no healing period, and can be replaced or removed later.

What it does not do is add gum tissue or address the cause. Composite placed at the gumline also needs maintaining well, because a rough or overhanging margin in that exact location is itself a risk to the tissue around it. The practice's page on [composite fillings](/procedures/restoration/composite-fillings) covers the material. For many people with mild recession, bonding plus a brushing change is the whole of the treatment.

### Gum Grafting

Grafting is the only way to put tissue back over an exposed root. The [American Academy of Periodontology describes gum graft surgery as covering the exposed root and helping to prevent additional recession and bone loss](https://www.perio.org/for-patients/periodontal-treatments-and-procedures/surgical-procedures/), with tissue taken from the palate or another donor source.

Several techniques exist. A connective tissue graft takes a layer from beneath the surface of the palate and places it over the root, usually under a flap of your own gum drawn upward to cover it. A free gingival graft places a full-thickness piece of palatal tissue directly, more often to thicken tissue than to cover a root. A coronally advanced flap moves existing gum upward over the root, with or without a graft beneath it, and tunnelling techniques do the same through small access points rather than a full flap. Donor tissue and collagen matrices can substitute for palatal tissue, avoiding a second surgical site.

The Cochrane review *Root coverage procedures for treating localised and multiple recession-type defects*, updated by Chambrone and colleagues in 2018, drew on 48 randomised controlled trials in 1,227 adults and concluded that several procedures can be used, with subepithelial connective tissue grafts showing a slight improvement where both root coverage and a gain in keratinized tissue are wanted. The [gum grafting page](/procedures/periodontal-services/gum-grafting) sets out the procedure in more detail.

## How Well Does Gum Grafting Actually Work?

This is the question patients ask and articles rarely answer with a number, so here are the numbers. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Periodontology 2000* pooled [32 randomised controlled trials covering 1,012 patients and 3,589 recession defects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428093/) in people with multiple adjacent recessions.

| Outcome | Pooled result | Confidence interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean root coverage | 82.6% | 71.3 – 93.9 |
| Complete root coverage | 62.7% of cases | 57.0 – 68.4 |
| Recession depth reduction | 2.22 mm | — |
| Keratinised tissue gain | 0.74 mm | 0.59 – 0.89 |
| Gum thickness gain | 0.56 mm | 0.43 – 0.68 |
| Post-operative pain (0–10 scale) | 2.67 | 2.28 – 3.06 |

Read those figures honestly. Most cases get most of the root covered, around six in ten get all of it, and roughly four in ten do not — which is the number to have in mind before surgery rather than after it. The same review found the best aesthetic results came from coronally advanced flaps and tunnel techniques used with a connective tissue graft or a graft substitute, ahead of pinhole and free gingival graft approaches.

One finding there is unusually reassuring. Patient-rated aesthetic satisfaction was high, pooling at 8.59 out of 10, and showed no meaningful correlation with how much root was actually covered. Patients whose coverage was incomplete were, on the whole, still happy with the result. Complete coverage is the clinical target. It is not the threshold for a good outcome.

A separate 2024 meta-analysis in *Dentistry Journal*, covering [612 patients and 1,708 recession sites](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11592634/), compared the coronally advanced flap with the tunnel technique and found the tunnel technique with a connective tissue graft gave significantly better mean root coverage than the flap with a graft, while the flap produced more keratinized tissue gain; the authors also note the tunnel's advantages in appearance and healing. There is no single correct technique, and the choice is properly made at the chair after the site has been examined.

## What Is Recovery From Gum Grafting Like?

Grafting is done under local anesthetic, and most people describe the days afterwards as uncomfortable rather than painful. The pooled post-operative pain score in the *Periodontology 2000* analysis was 2.67 on a ten-point scale, consistent with what patients report: manageable with ordinary pain relief. Where tissue has been taken from the palate, the palate is usually the sore part rather than the graft site — one reason donor tissue and collagen matrices are attractive for larger cases. Expect a soft diet at first, and expect to be told not to brush the surgical site for a period, using a prescribed rinse instead. Most people are back to normal function within a couple of weeks, with the tissue continuing to mature and blend for several months after that.

What happens afterwards decides the long-term result. A graft placed over a root still being scrubbed with a firm brush, still loaded by grinding, or still surrounded by inflammation has to survive the conditions that caused the problem in the first place. Ongoing [periodontal maintenance](/procedures/periodontal-services/maintenance) at the interval you are given is not an upsell after gum surgery. It is the thing that protects the work.

## How Do You Stop Recession Getting Worse?

Prevention here is not generic oral health advice. It is specific to which cause you have, which is why the diagnosis comes first.

If inflammation is driving it, the answer is thorough plaque control at the gumline and cleanings at the interval your risk warrants rather than a default one. If mechanical trauma is driving it, the answer is a soft brush, light pressure and a technique that angles bristles at the gumline instead of sawing across it. If grinding is driving it, the load needs managing. If it is anatomy, the answer is careful monitoring and early intervention if it starts to move, not more effort at home care that is already good enough.

Two things apply to everyone. Stop smoking if you smoke, given the doubled odds above and the effect on healing. And have the recession measured at the same sites each time, so that "it looks a bit worse" becomes a number instead of an impression.

### Questions Worth Asking at Your Next Visit

Bring these, because they turn a vague worry into a plan:

- How many millimeters of recession do I have, and on which teeth?
- Has it changed since my last recorded measurement?
- What do you think is causing mine?
- Is there active gum disease that needs treating first?
- Given the cause, is monitoring reasonable, or would you treat it now?
- If grafting were done, what coverage would you realistically expect on my case?

## When Should You Have Recession Looked At?

Sooner than most people do, because the useful window is while the cause can still be removed rather than after the tissue has gone.

Book an assessment if a tooth has visibly lengthened, if one spot is consistently sensitive to cold or air, if you can feel a notch or ledge at the gumline, if gums bleed when you brush, if any tooth feels loose or has moved, or if you have had recession pointed out before and have no idea whether it has progressed since. Bleeding and looseness in particular point toward active disease rather than simple mechanical wear, and those should not wait for a routine recall. Recession does not resolve itself, and the whole of the [periodontal services](/procedures/periodontal-services) side of dentistry works far better applied early.

Recession is sometimes a symptom of gum disease and sometimes entirely separate. If yours came with bleeding or deeper pockets, the [stages of gum disease](/blog/gum-disease-stages) explain what is happening underneath, and [when to see a gum specialist](/blog/when-to-see-a-gum-specialist) covers the referral threshold.

## Have Your Recession Measured Before You Decide Anything

Every question above resolves to the same two facts: how many millimeters you have lost, and what caused it. Those two answers decide whether you need nothing but a review date, a change to how you brush, treatment of active gum disease, a bonded repair, or a graft. It is a short appointment, and it replaces guesswork with a number you can track.
