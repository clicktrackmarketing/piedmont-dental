# Veneers or Crowns? Choosing the Right Fix for a Damaged Front Tooth

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

A veneer is the right answer when the front of a tooth looks wrong but the tooth itself is still structurally sound: a chip confined to the enamel, staining that will not lighten, a small gap, a worn edge. A crown is the right answer when the tooth has lost real structure — a fracture running through it, a large old filling doing the holding, or a tooth that has already been rebuilt once or twice. The dividing line is not how bad the tooth looks. It is how much healthy tooth is still there, and specifically how much enamel is left for a restoration to bond to.

### Key Takeaways

- A veneer resurfaces the front of a tooth. A crown encircles the whole tooth and holds it together.
- The decision is made on structure, not appearance. Surface damage points to a veneer; structural loss points to a crown.
- Veneers bonded entirely to enamel survive better than veneers bonded onto exposed dentine, and the gap is measurable.
- A crown removes far more tooth than a veneer, and that removal is permanent. Starting with a veneer keeps the crown option open; the reverse is not true.
- The published longevity figures do not support the common claim that a crown simply lasts longer than a veneer.
- Crowns are normally billed as major restorative work. Veneers placed for appearance are normally treated as cosmetic and are often not covered at all.

## Overview

Something has happened to a front tooth. Perhaps it chipped on a fork, perhaps a hairline crack has appeared that catches the light, perhaps an old bonded repair has stained at the edges and stopped matching. You searched for the fix and found two words used almost interchangeably, along with a lot of advice that boils down to *crowns are stronger, veneers are prettier*. That framing is not wrong, but it is not the decision a dentist is actually making when they look at your tooth.

This guide covers what physically separates the two restorations, the four questions a dentist works through to choose between them, why the enamel left on your tooth matters more than any other factor, what the published survival data genuinely says about longevity, what each procedure involves, how the two are priced and covered, and whether you can change your mind later. Where a topic already has its own guide on this site, it is linked rather than repeated.

## What Is Actually Different About a Veneer and a Crown?

The clearest way to hold the difference in mind is to think about coverage. A veneer is a thin shell bonded to the front surface of a tooth, a little like a false fingernail. A crown is a hollow replica of the tooth that fits down over the whole of it, like a thimble over a fingertip.

That single geometric difference produces every other difference between them. Because a veneer only faces forward, it relies on the tooth underneath to stay in one piece; it improves the surface without reinforcing anything. Because a crown wraps all the way around, it grips the tooth and resists the forces that would otherwise split it apart. The [American Dental Association](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/crowns) lists exactly this among the reasons crowns are used: to strengthen a tooth with a large filling when there is not enough tooth remaining, and to protect a weak tooth from breaking or restore one already broken. It is equally direct about the limit of a veneer, noting that veneers "cover only the front surface of a tooth rather than the entire tooth structure." That sentence is the whole comparison in miniature.

| | Porcelain veneer | Crown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it covers | The front surface, usually wrapping the biting edge | The entire tooth above the gumline |
| What it is for | Appearance of an intact tooth | Structure of a compromised tooth |
| Tooth removed | A thin layer, mostly from the front | Substantially more, from every surface |
| Reinforces the tooth | No | Yes |
| Category of dentistry | Cosmetic | Restorative |
| Reversible | No — enamel does not grow back | No |

### How Much Tooth Each One Removes

This is where the two options genuinely part company, and it is the part patients are least often told clearly. Preparing a tooth for a veneer means reducing the front and sides by a thin margin so the shell sits flush rather than proud. Preparing the same tooth for a crown means reducing every surface, including the back and the biting edge, so a full covering can be seated over what is left.

Neither is reversible. The ADA states the point plainly on its [veneers page](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/veneers): "Treatment is not reversible because tooth enamel is removed to place a veneer." That applies with more force to a crown, where more tooth is taken away — once a tooth has been prepared for a crown, it will need a crown for the rest of its life.

### What Each One Is Made Of

Both are usually ceramic, but not the same ceramics. Veneers are commonly feldspathic porcelain, leucite-reinforced glass ceramic or lithium disilicate; crowns for front teeth are usually lithium disilicate or zirconia, occasionally porcelain fused to metal, chosen for strength as much as for appearance.

Material is not a detail. A 2024 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry*, [covering 29 studies and 7,753 anterior ceramic laminate veneers in 986 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12076113/), found survival at a mean of 10.4 years that was broadly similar across materials — 96.13% for feldspathic, 96.81% for lithium disilicate, 93.7% for leucite-reinforced — but complication rates that were not. Technical complications ran at 41.48% for feldspathic veneers against 6.1% for lithium disilicate. The restorations mostly stayed in the mouth; some materials simply needed far more attention along the way. Ask which material is proposed for your tooth, and why.

## How Does a Dentist Actually Decide?

The triage is not mysterious and it is not primarily aesthetic. It runs through four questions in roughly this order, and the first one that returns the wrong answer settles it.

### 1. Is the Damage on the Surface or Through the Tooth?

A chip that has taken off a corner of enamel is a surface problem. A crack that you can trace from the biting edge up towards the gum, or a tooth that aches when you bite and releases, is a structural problem. A veneer laid over a fracture does not stop the fracture; it hides it while it continues to propagate. A crown, by contrast, binds the tooth circumferentially so that biting forces are shared rather than driven into the crack.

### 2. How Much Enamel Is Left to Bond To?

A veneer is held on by adhesion, and adhesion to enamel is stronger and more durable than adhesion to the softer dentine underneath. If previous work, wear or decay has already exposed a large area of dentine on the front of the tooth, the surface a veneer would have to grip is no longer the surface veneers perform best on. This factor is important enough that it has its own section below.

### 3. Has the Tooth Already Been Rebuilt Once?

A front tooth carrying a large old composite repair, or one that has been re-bonded several times, has less original structure than it appears to from the front. Once a substantial proportion of the visible tooth is filling material rather than tooth, a veneer is being bonded largely to old restorative work rather than to the tooth, and a crown becomes the more predictable option. Teeth that have had root canal treatment sit in this group too — they have lost internal structure and have historically shown higher failure rates under both types of restoration.

### 4. What Is the Bite Doing to That Tooth?

An upper front tooth that takes a heavy hit from the lower teeth every time you close, or a tooth in a mouth that grinds at night, is working under load that a thin bonded shell may not tolerate. Grinding shortens the life of both restorations, but it disadvantages a veneer more, because a veneer relies on a bond line that shear forces can break. Bring this up at your consultation if you wake with a sore jaw or have been told you clench.

| What is wrong with the tooth | Usually points to |
| --- | --- |
| Deep stain that whitening will not shift | Veneer |
| Small chip in the enamel, tooth otherwise sound | Veneer or bonding |
| Worn or uneven biting edge, structure intact | Veneer |
| Small gap or mildly rotated tooth | Veneer |
| Crack running into the body of the tooth | Crown |
| Large existing filling holding the tooth together | Crown |
| Tooth that has had root canal treatment | Crown |
| Substantial loss of the biting edge or a corner | Crown |
| Tooth broken at or below the gumline | Neither — a different conversation |

## Why Enamel Decides More Than Anything Else

If you take one technical point away from this guide, make it this one, because it is the mechanism behind the whole surface-versus-structure rule and it is almost never explained to patients.

A study published in the *Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry* in 2025 followed [672 ceramic veneers in 189 patients for between one and fifteen years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12618969/), with a mean follow-up of just under six years, and sorted them by how much dentine had been exposed during preparation. Veneers bonded to enamel only had an estimated survival rate of 96.7%. Where less than 30% of the bonding surface was dentine, survival was 95.3%. Where more than 30% was dentine, survival fell to 93.9%, and the difference from the enamel-only group was statistically significant. The authors calculated the odds of failure with any dentinal exposure at 3.47 times that of an enamel-only preparation.

The same pattern shows up in the failure modes. A 2021 systematic review in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine* pooling [25 clinical studies and roughly 6,500 porcelain laminate veneers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7961608/) found a ten-year cumulative survival rate of 95.5%, and reported that bonding to dentine is understood to be weaker than bonding to enamel, carrying a higher risk of microleakage and debonding. It also found that veneers designed with incisal coverage — wrapping over the biting edge rather than stopping short of it — failed less often than those without.

This is why "how much enamel is left?" is a better question than "how bad does it look?" A heavily stained but structurally untouched tooth is an excellent veneer case. A modest-looking chip on a tooth already drilled and rebuilt twice may not be.

## Which One Lasts Longer?

Almost every comparison you will read states that crowns last longer than veneers. It is repeated so consistently that it reads as settled. The published data is considerably less tidy than that, and the honest answer is that the two have not been measured in a way that allows a clean verdict.

### What the Veneer Data Shows

The pooled clinical evidence for porcelain veneers is unusually good for a cosmetic restoration. The 2021 systematic review above reports a ten-year cumulative survival rate of 95.5% across 6,500 veneers. When individual causes of failure were isolated, the ten-year survival figures were 96.3% against fracture, 99.2% against debonding, 99.3% against secondary decay and 99.0% against the need for further internal treatment. The 2024 meta-analysis gives a pooled survival rate of 97.12% at about five years, based on 3,761 veneers in studies with four to six years of follow-up.

### What the Crown Data Shows

A retrospective clinical study in the *European Journal of Oral Sciences* in 2022 followed [1,037 tooth-supported single crowns placed for 401 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9546353), with a mean follow-up of just over eleven years. Cumulative survival was 89.9% at five years and 80.9% at ten years, falling to 70.5% at fifteen years and 61.8% at twenty. The leading causes of failure were loss of retention, loss of the tooth itself, and tooth fracture.

Three findings in that study matter directly for a front tooth. Crowns placed on anterior teeth failed more often than crowns placed on back teeth. Crowns on teeth that had had root canal treatment failed more often than crowns on vital teeth. And patients who grind failed at significantly higher rates. A crown on a front tooth is not the easy, durable default it is often presented as.

### Why the Two Sets of Numbers Are Not Directly Comparable

Set side by side, the veneer figures look better than the crown figures. Ending the section there would be misleading, and the reason is worth understanding.

| Restoration | Survival reported | Source and sample |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Porcelain laminate veneers | 95.5% at 10 years | Systematic review, 25 studies, ~6,500 veneers, 2021 |
| Anterior ceramic laminate veneers | 97.12% at ~5 years (3,761 veneers); 93.7–96.81% at ~10 years by material | Meta-analysis, 29 studies, 7,753 veneers, 2024 |
| Ceramic veneers, enamel-only bonding | 96.7% | 672 veneers, 189 patients, 1–15 years, 2025 |
| Ceramic veneers, >30% dentine exposed | 93.9% | Same study |
| Tooth-supported single crowns | 89.9% at 5 years; 80.9% at 10; 70.5% at 15; 61.8% at 20 | Retrospective clinical study, 1,037 crowns, 401 patients, 2022 |

The veneer numbers are pooled from clinical studies, many run in university or specialist settings on carefully selected, structurally sound teeth. The crown numbers come from one long-running practice-based series in which crowns were placed on teeth already damaged enough to need one. The populations are not the same, so the comparison measures the teeth as much as the restorations.

The fair summary is that both are durable, both are routinely still in service after a decade, and the tooth underneath drives the outcome more than the choice between them. What the data does not support is the idea that choosing a crown buys you longevity. If anything it suggests the opposite: the situations calling for a crown are the situations with the poorer outlook, whatever you place on them.

## What Does Each Procedure Involve?

Both are usually two-visit procedures, and the sequence is broadly similar, but what happens in the mouth is not.

### Getting a Veneer

The first visit covers diagnosis, shade selection and preparation: a thin reduction of the front and sides, an impression or digital scan, and usually a temporary veneer while the permanent one is made. The second is try-in and bonding. Shade is chosen before any preparation begins and is worth taking seriously, because ceramic does not respond to whitening later — if you intend to lighten your other teeth, [professional whitening](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry/tooth-whitening) belongs before the veneer, not after.

### Getting a Crown

The first visit involves more extensive preparation, since every surface has to be reduced. Any decay or failing filling is removed first, and the tooth may need building up internally before it can carry a crown at all. A temporary crown is fitted and the permanent one cemented at the second visit. Some crowns can be designed and milled in a single visit using CEREC® technology, covered in our guide to [same-day crowns](/blog/cerec-same-day-crowns-oakland) and on the [CEREC page](/procedures/cosmetic-dentistry/cerec).

### Where Bonding Fits In

For the smallest chips there is a third option that is neither of these: direct composite bonding, sculpted onto the tooth in one appointment with little or no preparation. It costs less, is repairable and is the most conservative choice available, but it stains more readily and does not last as long as porcelain. Because it changes the calculation for minor damage, it has its own comparison — see [porcelain veneers versus dental bonding](/blog/porcelain-veneers-vs-dental-bonding) rather than treating this guide as the last word on small chips.

## What Do They Cost?

Fees vary widely by region, by material, by laboratory and by how much preparatory work the tooth needs, so treat every published figure as a market range rather than a quote. Only an examination produces a real number for your tooth.

### How Insurance Treats Each One

This is where the two options genuinely diverge financially, and it often matters more than the sticker price. Crowns are classified as major restorative work. The [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/) lists crowns among the major procedures typically reimbursed at the lowest percentage, such as 50%, and notes that about 65% of dental PPOs carry an annual maximum benefit of $1,500 or more.

Veneers are treated differently. [Delta Dental states](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/procedures/veneers/cost-and-insurance-coverage/) that veneers "are commonly considered a cosmetic procedure and are often not covered by employer, family, or individual dental insurance plans." The practical effect is that a crown and a veneer with similar list prices can produce very different out-of-pocket totals. Ask your own plan for a pre-treatment estimate on whichever option is proposed before you commit, and check where you stand against your annual maximum.

## Can You Change Your Mind Later?

Partly, and the asymmetry is worth planning around. A tooth prepared for a veneer can still be crowned later: the preparation is conservative, most of the tooth remains, and if the veneer fails or the tooth deteriorates, a crown is still available as the next step. That is a genuine argument for taking the more conservative option when a case sits on the line between the two.

A tooth prepared for a crown cannot realistically go back to a veneer. Too much has been removed for a thin shell to have anything to bond to or to cover. A crown can be replaced with another crown, and typically will be at some point, but the tooth is committed. That is the strongest reason to be certain before choosing the more invasive option, and a reasonable basis for seeking a second opinion when a crown is recommended for a tooth that looks intact to you.

Neither restoration is permanent. The ADA is explicit that a veneer "may chip, crack, wear down or loosen over time" and may need re-bonding, repair or replacement, and the survival figures above show crowns being replaced too. Plan for maintenance rather than for a final answer.

## How Each One Looks, and Where Each One Struggles

On appearance alone, a well-made veneer usually has the advantage on a front tooth. Because it is thin and bonded to a translucent tooth, light passes through it in something close to the way it passes through enamel, which is what makes a restored tooth read as real rather than as opaque. A crown has an opaque core to hide, and matching a single crown to its natural neighbors is one of the harder things in dentistry — particularly on an upper central incisor, where the eye has an identical tooth beside it for comparison.

Each struggles somewhere different. A veneer struggles at the margins and under load: it can debond, chip at the edge, or leave a visible line at the gum if the gum recedes. A crown struggles beneath itself, where decay at the margin and fracture of the underlying tooth are recurring failure modes that develop quietly because the tooth is hidden. Both need the same daily care as natural teeth, and both benefit from examinations that check the margins rather than only the surface.

## What to Ask at Your Consultation

A good consultation for a damaged front tooth should leave you able to explain the reasoning back in your own words rather than simply accepting a recommendation. A short list of questions gets you there.

- How much healthy tooth structure is left, and how much enamel remains on the surface that would be bonded?
- Is there a crack, and how far into the tooth does it run?
- Which material is proposed, and why that one for this tooth?
- Will the design cover the biting edge?
- What happens if it fails, and what does the repair path involve?
- Can I see photographs of comparable cases where a single front tooth was restored?

Then ask the question that matters most: what would the other option look like on this tooth, and what rules it out? If a crown is recommended, ask specifically what disqualifies a veneer. If a veneer is recommended, ask what would make you a crown case instead. A clear answer is the best evidence you have that the triage was done properly rather than by habit. ## Talk Through Your Options for a Damaged Front Tooth

If a front tooth has chipped, cracked or stopped matching its neighbors, the most useful next step is an examination that establishes how much sound tooth is left. That is the finding the whole decision rests on, and it is not something you can judge from a mirror or a photograph. A consultation should leave you understanding why one option was recommended over the other, and what would change the recommendation.
