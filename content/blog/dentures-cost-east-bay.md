# What Dentures Cost in the East Bay — And What Changes the Number

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

Published market data puts a conventional full denture at roughly $1,115 to $2,540 per arch and a partial denture at roughly $1,425 to $2,785 per arch, using Delta Dental's 2024 out-of-network averages. Other national surveys run wider, from about $1,000 to $3,000 for a full denture and $800 to $3,100 for a partial depending on the material. Those figures cover the denture itself and nothing else, and the items around it — extractions, imaging, tissue conditioning, adjustments, relines and eventual replacement — are what actually separate one quote from another. There is no official national fee list to check any of it against, because the American Dental Association discontinued its Survey of Dental Fees in 2023. Bay Area fees run above these national figures: at Piedmont Dental By Design, dentures and partials are $2,976 to $3,173 depending on the arch, and current fees are on the [restorative dentistry](/procedures/restoration) page.

### Key Takeaways

- A denture is quoted per arch, so a full upper and lower set is close to double a single-arch price.
- The material changes the number more than almost anything else: acrylic, cast metal and flexible nylon partials sit in different price brackets and behave differently over time.
- Extractions are the single largest add-on, and a mouth needing several of them can cost more in extractions than in the denture.
- Relines and remakes are scheduled costs, not defects, because the bone ridge under a denture keeps resorbing after the teeth come out.
- Most dental plans treat dentures as a major service, typically paid at around 50% and capped by an annual maximum that a denture can consume outright.
- Original Medicare does not cover dentures at all, which surprises a large share of the people who need them most.

## Overview

If you have collected two or three denture quotes and found them thousands of dollars apart, the difference is rarely the denture. It is usually what each quote assumed about your mouth: how many teeth still need removing, whether the tissues need conditioning first, what the base and the teeth are made from, and whether the price includes the follow-up visits where the fit is corrected. A denture is one of the few dental treatments where the item you are buying is a small part of the total, and where the cost continues quietly for as long as you wear it.

This guide takes the number apart. It covers why no authoritative fee list exists any more, what published ranges say for each denture type, which materials move the price and why, what gets added to a treatment plan, why relining and remaking are inevitable rather than optional, how dental insurance behaves, and what to ask before you accept a quote in the East Bay.

## Why Is There No Official Price for a Denture?

Because the one national fee benchmark patients used to be able to check has been withdrawn. The [American Dental Association's Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market) states that the Council on Dental Practice discontinued the Survey of Dental Fees in 2023 following a change in law, and that the ADA cannot quote fees for dental procedures and is forbidden by federal law from setting or recommending them.

That matters when you are reading price guides. Every range published online now — including the ones in this article — is assembled from insurer claims data, provider surveys or aggregated market observation. Those are useful, and they are not the same thing as an official fee schedule. Treat any single figure presented as "the average cost of dentures" with a degree of caution, and pay more attention to the spread and to what the range is said to include.

The other reason to check the date on any figure you find is that dental prices move. The same ADA Health Policy Institute page reports national dental care expenditures of $189 billion in 2024, up 3.6% on 2023 after adjusting for inflation, with consumer dental spending up 4% in the twelve months to January 2026. A quote or a published range more than a year old is worth refreshing before you budget against it.

## What Does a Denture Cost, by Type?

Dentures are priced per arch — one upper or one lower — and by how much of the arch they replace. The table below sets two independent published sources side by side rather than averaging them, because they disagree at the edges and the disagreement is informative.

| Denture type | Delta Dental, 2024 out-of-network average | Authority Dental, national price survey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full upper denture | $1,220 — $2,540 | $1,000 — $3,000 (traditional, either arch) |
| Full lower denture | $1,115 — $2,425 | $1,000 — $3,000 (traditional, either arch) |
| Immediate full denture | Not separately listed | $1,000 — $3,500 |
| Interim or temporary full denture | Not separately listed | $300 — $2,000 |
| Partial upper denture | $1,435 — $2,785 | $800 — $3,100 depending on material |
| Partial lower denture | $1,425 — $2,490 | $800 — $3,100 depending on material |
| Interim or temporary partial ("flipper") | Not separately listed | $450 — $1,100 |

[Delta Dental's denture cost page](https://www.deltadental.com/protect-my-smile/procedures/dentures/cost-and-insurance-coverage/) publishes those first ranges as out-of-network averages and describes what they normally include: the impressions, the fabrication of a custom denture, and the adjustments needed to get the fit right, along with follow-up realignment visits in most cases. [Authority Dental's price survey](https://www.authoritydental.org/how-much-do-dentures-cost) runs wider at both ends because it separates the economy and premium tiers that the insurer averages fold together.

Economy dentures sit at the bottom of those ranges, and they are a legitimate product for the right situation. They are generally made from stock teeth on a standard base with fewer fitting appointments, which is exactly how the price comes down, and what you trade is fit precision, aesthetics and the number of chair visits available to correct problems. The honest way to compare an economy quote with a mid-range one is to ask both providers how many fitting appointments are included and what an additional visit costs, because that is where the two prices actually differ.

### Why a Full Set Is Not Simply Two Prices Added Together

An upper and a lower denture are two separate appliances, so the arithmetic starts at roughly double. In practice the two arches are rarely equally straightforward. An upper denture is held by suction across the palate and generally settles well. A lower denture has no palate to grip, sits on a narrower horseshoe of bone and has to share the space with your tongue, which is why lower dentures are the ones people struggle with and the ones that come back for adjustment more often. If a treatment plan quotes a single "full set" figure, ask how it splits, because the follow-up work usually does not split evenly.

## Which Materials Change the Price, and by How Much?

More than any other single factor, once extractions are out of the picture. Partial dentures in particular come in three material families that cost differently, last differently and — importantly for the long-term bill — can be repaired and relined to very different degrees. The ranges below are Authority Dental's published national figures for each type.

| Partial denture type | What it is | Published range | The trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acrylic resin | A pink acrylic base carrying the replacement teeth | $800 — $2,700 | Cheapest and easiest to add to; bulkier and less rigid |
| Cast metal framework | A thin cobalt-chromium skeleton with acrylic and teeth on top | $1,300 — $3,100 | Strongest and thinnest; metal clasps can show |
| Flexible nylon | A thermoplastic base that flexes rather than fracturing | $900 — $2,500 | No visible metal; the hardest of the three to reline or repair |

### Acrylic Resin Partials

Acrylic is the default denture base material, and has been since the 1940s. It is straightforward to make, straightforward to add a tooth to when another one is lost, and straightforward to reline. The cost of that simplicity is bulk: an acrylic partial has to be thicker to be strong enough, which most wearers notice for the first few weeks.

### Cast Metal Framework Partials

A cast metal partial uses a slim cobalt-chromium framework to carry the load, which allows a thinner, more rigid and generally more comfortable appliance that transmits chewing force to the teeth rather than the gum. It costs more, largely because the framework is individually cast, and the [2023 review of denture base materials in *Polymers*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10422349/) notes that cobalt-chromium alloys need specific equipment to work with because of their hardness. The visible trade-off is the clasps that hold it onto your remaining teeth.

### Flexible Nylon Partials

Flexible partials are the ones marketed on the absence of metal clasps, and they do look better in the smile zone. The same *Polymers* review is candid about the cost of that: polyamides are challenging to process and repair, absorb more water, are more prone to staining, and are not frequently used as denture base materials except in specific cases. The practical consequence is financial. A partial that cannot easily be relined when the ridge changes is a partial that gets replaced instead of adjusted, and replacement is a great deal more expensive than a reline.

### The Teeth Themselves

Denture teeth are a separate line in the laboratory bill. Basic acrylic teeth are the standard; higher-grade layered acrylic and composite teeth cost more and look considerably more natural, particularly at the front. Porcelain teeth are harder and hold their shape well but are heavier and can wear the opposing natural teeth, which is why they are used selectively rather than by default. If aesthetics matter to you, this is the part of the quote worth asking about by name, because it is a genuine choice rather than an upsell.

### Do Digital or Same-Day Dentures Cost Less?

Not reliably, on the current evidence. A [2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *Journal of Prosthodontics*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12990028/), pooling five studies covering 184 patients and 202 removable complete dentures, found no statistically significant difference between digital and conventional workflows in laboratory cost, clinical cost, total cost, or the number of treatment sessions. The authors concluded that the two approaches are comparable on cost-efficiency and that the evidence is not yet sufficient to establish an advantage either way. Digital fabrication has real benefits — a stored design can be reprinted if a denture is lost or broken — but a lower price is not one you should assume.

## What Gets Added to the Denture Price?

This is where two quotes genuinely diverge. The denture is a single line; the treatment plan around it is several, and the ranges below come from Authority Dental's published price survey.

| Line item | When it applies | Published range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral examination | Almost always, at the start | $50 — $200 |
| Panoramic X-ray | To assess remaining teeth, roots and bone | $100 — $250 |
| Diagnostic casts | Planning models of your arches | $50 — $300 |
| Tooth extraction | Each remaining tooth that has to come out | $200 — $700 per tooth |
| Tissue conditioning | Soft liner while gums settle after surgery | $100 — $450 |
| Denture adjustment | Sore spots and fit corrections after fitting | $50 — $250 each |
| Chairside reline | Refitting the base to a changed ridge, in one visit | $200 — $500 |
| Laboratory reline | A more substantial refit sent to the laboratory | $350 — $900 |

Extractions are the biggest single add-on of those. If teeth still need removing, that is usually the largest number on the plan after the denture itself: at a published $200 to $700 per tooth, a mouth needing eight or ten removals can carry more cost in extractions than in the appliance that replaces them. Surgical extractions — broken teeth, retained roots, anything that needs to be sectioned — sit higher again. Any quote for "dentures" that does not state the number of extractions it assumes is not a quote you can compare with another one.

### The Immediate Denture Trap

An immediate denture is fitted on the same day the remaining teeth are removed, so you never appear in public without teeth. That is a real benefit and it is why many people choose one. The cost consequence is the part that gets skipped in the consultation. [The ADA's MouthHealthy guide to dentures](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/dentures) describes an immediate denture as one inserted on the same day the remaining teeth come out, and notes that it may need relining or remaking afterwards as the tissues heal — whereas a conventional denture is made and placed only after the tissues have healed, which may take several months.

Read that as a budget line, not a footnote. Choosing an immediate denture often means paying for a substantial reline, or in some cases a second definitive denture, within the first year. That can still be the right choice. It is only a bad one when nobody told you it was coming.

## Why Do Dentures Need Relining and Remaking at All?

Because the bone they rest on does not stay the same shape. When a tooth is removed, the ridge of bone that held it begins to resorb, and the process does not stop once the socket has healed over.

The measured rates are worth knowing. Tan and colleagues' systematic review of post-extraction dimensional changes in humans, published in *Clinical Oral Implants Research*, found horizontal bone loss of 29% to 63% and vertical loss of 11% to 22% within six months of extraction, with ridge width reducing by up to 50% in the first year and roughly two-thirds of that happening inside the first three months. After that early collapse, resorption continues slowly for as long as the ridge carries a denture. A [2016 systematic review in the *International Journal of Implant Dentistry*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5120622/), drawing on 18 studies, reports that annual alveolar bone resorption in denture wearers runs roughly fourfold higher in the lower jaw than in the upper.

Two practical consequences follow. The first is that a denture which fitted beautifully at delivery will loosen, and the timing is biology rather than workmanship. The second is that the lower denture loosens faster, which is exactly what long-term wearers report. Relines, and eventually a remake, are the maintenance schedule for an appliance sitting on a moving foundation. Budget for them from the beginning rather than treating them as an unwelcome surprise, and see the [dentures and partial dentures page](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) for how the appliance itself is planned and fitted.

## How Long Should a Denture Last Before You Pay Again?

Long enough to be worth doing well, and not as long as most people assume. Published survival data for removable partial dentures is the most useful guide available, and it is sobering: a review of the epidemiological evidence in the *Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research* records reported partial denture survival of around [75% at five years and 50% at ten years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4948543/), citing earlier longitudinal work. The same review identifies loss of a supporting tooth as the primary reason partials are replaced, accounting for 56% of design changes in the lower arch.

That last point is the one to carry into a decision. A partial denture clasps onto the teeth you still have, and those teeth carry additional load and are harder to keep clean around. If one of them is lost, the partial usually has to be remade rather than modified. Keeping the abutment teeth healthy is therefore the cheapest thing you can do for the lifetime cost of a partial.

| Cost event | When it typically arises | Published range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The denture itself | Once, at the start | $1,000 — $3,500 per arch, by type |
| Adjustments | Sore spots in the first weeks, then as needed | $50 — $250 each |
| Tissue conditioning | While tissues settle after extractions | $100 — $450 |
| Reline | Periodically, as the ridge resorbs | $200 — $900 |
| Repair after a fracture | Unpredictable | Varies with the break |
| Replacement | Around half of partials are replaced within ten years | The full cost again |

None of this makes a denture a poor choice. It makes it a decision with a ten-year cost rather than a one-off price, which is the only fair way to compare it with the alternatives.

## Does Dental Insurance Cover Dentures?

Partly, and the ceiling matters more than the percentage. Dentures are classified as a major restorative service on almost every plan, which puts them in the lowest-paying tier.

### How Much a Plan Typically Pays

[Delta Dental's guide to what dental insurance covers](https://www.deltadental.com/us/en/protect-my-smile/dental-insurance-101/what-does-my-dental-insurance-cover.html) sets out the standard structure: around 100% of preventive and diagnostic care, about 80% of basic procedures such as fillings and extractions, and roughly 50% of major procedures. Prosthodontic work sits in that last group. A deductible commonly applies to the non-preventive tiers, and there is usually a waiting period before major services become claimable on an individual policy.

### The Annual Maximum Is the Real Limit

This is what catches denture patients out. According to the [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/), about 65% of dental PPOs carry an annual maximum of $1,500 or more, and between 2014 and 2023 fewer than 5% of enrollees on average ever reached their cap. Annual maximums are set at a level almost nobody touches — which is precisely why they are set so low, and why a course of treatment involving extractions and two dentures can exhaust an entire year of benefit before the second arch is finished. Where treatment naturally spans a change of benefit year, two annual maximums can sometimes apply to one course of care, though that should be a clinical decision rather than a financial one.

### Frequency Limits and Waiting Periods

Plans also restrict how often they will pay for the same thing. Delta Dental describes these frequency limitations as time limits between services, giving the example of a policy paying for a full set of X-rays only once every three years. Denture replacement is normally subject to its own interval, so if you are replacing an existing denture rather than getting a first one, the question to ask your insurer is how many years must have passed since the last one was paid for.

### Medicare, Medi-Cal and Pre-Tax Dollars

Original Medicare is the gap that surprises people most. [Medicare's own coverage page](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/dental-services) states that it does not cover dental services such as routine cleanings, fillings and extractions, or items such as dentures and implants, and that you pay all costs for non-covered services. Some Medicare Advantage plans add a dental benefit, but it is a plan-by-plan question rather than a Medicare entitlement.

California's Medicaid program is different. According to [Disability Rights California's guide to dental services through Medi-Cal](https://www.disabilityrightsca.org/publications/dental-services-through-medi-cal), full dentures, partial dentures, and denture adjustments, repairs and relines are covered adult benefits, subject to prior authorization through a Treatment Authorization Request; adult dental services carry an $1,800 per-beneficiary calendar-year limit, from which dentures are exempt. Program rules change, so confirm current details with Medi-Cal Dental directly before planning around them.

Finally, a health savings account or flexible spending account lets you pay with pre-tax money, and Delta Dental notes that denture costs are often reimbursable through an HSA, HRA or FSA — worth confirming with your administrator first. The practice's [insurance](/resources/insurance) and [financing](/resources/financing) pages are the place to check before you plan around a benefit.

## What Does an Implant-Retained Denture Cost?

A different price class altogether, and the published guides disagree enough that both sets of figures are worth seeing. Some national cost guides put a snap-in overdenture — one that clips onto two or more implants and still comes out for cleaning — at roughly $3,000 to $7,300 per arch, with a fixed full-arch bridge from about $8,000. Bay Area fees run higher than those national guides, and the prosthetic material rather than the number of implants accounts for most of the spread; our guide to what dental implants cost in the East Bay sets out the local figures.

What you are buying at that price is stability and, materially, load. Because implants transmit chewing force into the bone, they slow the resorption that a conventional denture does nothing to prevent — the 2016 review cited above reports that implant-retained cases lose ridge bone more slowly than conventional dentures do. Whether that is worth the difference is a separate decision that turns on your bone volume, your general health and your budget horizon. The [dental implants page](/procedures/restoration/dental-implants) explains the procedure, and [What Dental Implants Really Cost in the East Bay](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay) itemizes the implant side of the comparison properly. If you still have healthy teeth on either side of a gap, a [fixed bridge](/procedures/restoration/fixed-bridges) is a third option worth pricing alongside a partial.

## Why the East Bay Number Differs From a National Average

Dental fees track local costs — premises, laboratory work, staffing — and the Bay Area sits above the national mean on all three. National ranges are therefore a starting point rather than a forecast. At our practice, dentures and partials are $2,976 to $3,173 depending on the arch; current fees are on the [restorative dentistry](/procedures/restoration) page.

If you want to sense-check a national figure against your area, [FAIR Health's consumer dental cost lookup](https://www.fairhealthconsumer.org/dental) estimates typical costs for a specific procedure in a specific location, which is the closest thing available to a local benchmark now that the ADA survey has gone.

There is also a broader reason this topic matters locally. [CDC data](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/data-research/facts-stats/fast-facts-tooth-loss.html) records that a quarter of adults aged 65 or older have eight or fewer teeth, and more than one in seven have lost all of them. Tooth replacement is not a niche problem, and the earlier it is planned, the more options remain open.

## What Should You Ask Before You Accept a Denture Quote?

Most of the unpleasant surprises in denture treatment trace back to a question nobody asked at the consultation. Write these down and take the answers with you.

1. Is this price per arch or for both arches?
2. How many extractions does the quote assume, and what does each additional one cost?
3. What is the base made of, and what grade are the teeth?
4. How many fitting and adjustment appointments are included, and what does an extra visit cost?
5. If this is an immediate denture, when will it need relining, and is that reline included?
6. What will a reline and a replacement cost later, at today's prices?
7. Will you submit a pre-treatment estimate to my insurer before I commit?

A practice that answers all seven plainly has given you a quote you can genuinely compare with another one. The seventh matters more than it looks: a pre-treatment estimate has your insurer state in advance what it will pay, which removes most of the guesswork from the part of the bill you cannot control. To see how a removable option is planned and fitted before you decide, the [restorative dentistry section](/procedures/restoration) and the [dentures resource page](/resources/restorations/dentures) both walk through the process.
