# What a Full-Mouth Reconstruction Costs in the East Bay

**Published by Piedmont Dental By Design | Piedmont, CA**

## Quick Answer

Published 2026 patient cost guides put a full-mouth reconstruction somewhere between roughly $15,000 and $90,000, and the range is that wide for an honest reason: a reconstruction is a plan, not a procedure. You are pricing a sequence of separate treatments — gum treatment, extractions, grafting, implants, crowns, sometimes a rebuilt bite — carried out in stages across roughly one to two years. Where a case lands in that range depends far more on how many teeth can be saved and whether the bite itself has to be rebuilt than on the price of any single item. No figure means much until a full set of records exists, and a written, phased plan is the only quote worth comparing.

### Key Takeaways

- A full-mouth reconstruction is a staged sequence of treatments, so it is quoted as a plan with many line items rather than as one fee.
- Published 2026 guides disagree at both ends of the range — $15,000 to $80,000 in one, $20,000 to $90,000 in another — which shows how loosely the term is used.
- There is no current authoritative national fee benchmark for dentistry, because the ADA's Survey of Dental Fees was discontinued in 2023, so every published figure is a market range rather than a survey average.
- The largest cost fork is whether teeth can be saved and restored or must be removed and replaced, because replacement brings surgery, healing time and prosthetics with it.
- Dental insurance typically contributes a small share of a large plan, because the annual maximum binds long before the coverage percentage does.
- Phasing treatment across two or three benefit years is the one lever that reliably increases what insurance pays, and it often suits the healing timeline anyway.

## Overview

If you have been told you need a full-mouth reconstruction, you have probably already searched for the price and found numbers that do not agree with each other by a factor of five. That is not because anyone is hiding the figure. It is because "full-mouth reconstruction" describes a scope of work rather than a procedure, and two people given that same phrase can be facing genuinely different treatment: one keeping most of their teeth and having them rebuilt, the other having every remaining tooth removed and replaced. The label is identical. The plans are not.

This guide takes the number apart the way a treatment plan does. It covers what a full-mouth plan usually includes, how the work is staged over months and why that sequence exists, what pushes a case toward the top or the bottom of the range, what dental insurance realistically pays against a plan this size, and what actually has to happen at a consultation before anyone can give you a real figure. Every cost below is a labeled national market range drawn from published patient guides; our own fees for the individual treatments are listed after the table.

## What Is a Full-Mouth Reconstruction, Exactly?

A full-mouth reconstruction — also called a full-mouth restoration or rehabilitation — is a coordinated plan to rebuild the function and appearance of an entire mouth, upper and lower, rather than fixing one tooth at a time. It usually becomes the right approach when several problems have arrived together: teeth lost or broken, years of wear, old crowns and fillings all reaching the end of their lives at once, and a bite that no longer closes the way it was designed to. You can see what that looks like in practice on the [full-mouth restoration case page](/resources/full-mouth-restoration), which shows completed cases from the practice, and the [restoration overview](/procedures/restoration) sets out the individual treatments a plan draws on.

The distinction that matters for pricing is between three things that get talked about interchangeably and cost very different amounts.

| Term | What it actually means | How it is priced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-mouth reconstruction | A staged plan combining whatever treatments the mouth needs, across both arches | Per line item, summed into a phased plan |
| Full-arch implants (All-on-4 and similar) | One fixed bridge carried by four to six implants, replacing every tooth in an arch | Per arch, as a single package |
| Cosmetic smile makeover | Appearance-led work, usually veneers or crowns on the visible front teeth | Per tooth, for the teeth that show when you smile |

A reconstruction can contain a full-arch implant case inside it — that is common when one arch is beyond saving — but the two are not the same purchase. Because full-arch pricing has its own logic, it is broken out separately in the [East Bay dental implant cost guide](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay) rather than repeated here.

Wear alone is a more common reason for reconstruction than most people expect. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Public Health in Practice*, [pooling 133 studies and 92,153 people](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12771503/), put the global prevalence of tooth wear at 40.8%. Grinding is part of why: a 2024 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine* [estimated global bruxism prevalence at 22.2%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11278015/), with sleep bruxism at 21% and awake bruxism at 23%. Most of that wear never needs rebuilding. When it does, it tends to need rebuilding everywhere at once, which is precisely how a mouth ends up needing a plan rather than a repair.

## Why Is the Price Range So Wide?

Because the phrase describes a bundle, and nobody agrees on what is in the bundle. Published cost guides are written by dental practices rather than by researchers, each describing the mix of cases they happen to see, so their ranges reflect their patient population as much as anything else.

| Source type | Range quoted for a full-mouth reconstruction |
| --- | --- |
| A 2026 cosmetic dentistry practice guide | $15,000 — $80,000+ |
| A second 2026 practice guide, split by scope | Moderate $15,000 — $40,000 · Extensive $45,000 — $80,000 |
| A 2026 restoration cost guide | $20,000 — $90,000, with complex implant-heavy cases above $100,000 |

Those are three honest answers to the same question, and they disagree by more than $50,000 at the top. It is worth knowing why no better source exists. The American Dental Association's *Survey of Dental Fees*, which for years was the reference point for what a procedure typically cost, was discontinued in 2023 and is no longer published. There is currently no authoritative national fee benchmark for dentistry, which means every figure you will read online — including the ones in this article — is a market range assembled from practice guides rather than a surveyed average.

What the ADA does still publish is the direction of travel. Its Health Policy Institute reports [national dental expenditure of $189 billion in 2024](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-care-market), up 3.6% on the previous year after adjusting for inflation, with consumer dental spending up 4% in the twelve months to January 2026. The practical consequence is simple: a quote or an online figure more than a year old is worth refreshing before you budget against it.

## What Does a Full-Mouth Plan Usually Include?

Almost never all of the following, and rarely fewer than four of them. The plan is assembled from whatever the mouth actually needs, which is why the itemized sheet is the document that matters and the headline total is the one that misleads.

| Element of the plan | When it appears | Typical market range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comprehensive exam, full-mouth X-rays, 3D scan | Almost always, at the diagnostic stage | $200 — $400 |
| Scaling and root planing, or periodontal surgery | Where gum disease has to be controlled first | $500 — $10,000 depending on extent |
| Filling | Decay removed from a tooth that is being kept | $90 — $450 per tooth |
| Simple extraction | A tooth that is still intact and accessible | $150 — $300 per tooth |
| Surgical extraction | Broken, impacted or root-fractured teeth | $250 — $600 per tooth |
| Bone grafting | Where bone volume is insufficient for an implant | $500 — $3,000 per site |
| Sinus lift | Upper back teeth with insufficient vertical bone | $1,500 — $5,000 |
| Dental implant, complete | Replacing an individual tooth root and crown | $3,000 — $6,000 nationally |
| Crown | Rebuilding a tooth that can be saved | $800 — $2,500 per tooth |
| Fixed bridge | Spanning a gap using the teeth either side | About $3,600 for a three-unit bridge, national average |
| Porcelain veneer | Front teeth needing shape and color, not structure | $1,000 — $2,500 per tooth |
| Full-arch implant bridge | An entire arch replaced on four to six implants | $15,000 — $38,000 per arch by material |
| Conventional or implant-retained denture | Where a removable option is the right answer | $1,000 — $5,000 · $6,000 — $20,000 per arch |

Those are national market ranges, and Bay Area fees run above them. At Piedmont Dental By Design, a single implant starts at $8,474; a porcelain crown on a back tooth is $1,949 to $2,351 and front teeth start at $2,379; a typical three-unit bridge is $5,847 to $6,651; porcelain veneers start at $2,379 per tooth; composite fillings are $361 to $565; and dentures and partials are $2,976 to $3,173 depending on the arch. Current fees are on the [restorative dentistry](/procedures/restoration) page, and the [East Bay implant cost breakdown](/blog/dental-implant-cost-east-bay) sets out the implant side of a plan in more detail. Two further items belong on this list even though they resist a range: root canal treatment, where a tooth being kept has a compromised nerve, and orthodontic movement, where teeth have drifted into the space a restoration needs. Both are common in reconstructions and both are frequently missing from the first estimate someone is given.

## How Is a Full-Mouth Reconstruction Staged?

This is the part almost no cost guide explains, and it is the part that determines both your timeline and your cash flow. A reconstruction is not one long appointment. It is four phases, and each phase has to finish — including its healing — before the next one can be planned properly.

| Stage | What happens | Typical duration | What you are paying for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Records and diagnosis | Exam, imaging, periodontal charting, photographs, models, bite assessment | 1 — 2 visits over 2 — 4 weeks | Diagnostics and the plan itself |
| 2. Disease control | Gum treatment, decay removed, hopeless teeth extracted, temporary comfort restored | Weeks to a few months | Periodontal treatment, fillings, extractions |
| 3. Foundation work | Grafting where needed, implants placed, then left to heal | 3 — 9 months, mostly healing | Surgery, grafts, implant components |
| 4. Provisionals, then definitive restorations | Temporary teeth worn and assessed, then the final crowns and bridges made and fitted | 3 — 6 months | Laboratory work and the final restorations |

### 1. Records and Diagnosis

Nothing can be priced before this stage, and a plan produced without it is a guess. The records are a comprehensive examination, a full series of X-rays and usually a 3D scan, periodontal charting of every tooth, intraoral and facial photographs, and models of your teeth mounted so the way your jaw closes can be studied outside your mouth. Together they answer the two questions everything else depends on: which teeth have a future, and where the bite should sit. It is also the stage at which a diagnostic wax-up or digital design is made, showing the intended result before any tooth is touched.

### 2. Disease Control

Rebuilding on top of active disease is an expensive mistake, which is why gum treatment and decay removal come before anything permanent. Active periodontal disease progressively destroys the bone that holds teeth and implants alike, so it has to be brought under control and shown to be stable before restorative work is committed to. The [periodontal treatment page](/procedures/periodontal-services/treatment) explains what that stage involves. This phase is also where hopeless teeth come out, and where a re-evaluation appointment a few weeks later confirms the gums have actually responded before the plan moves on.

### 3. Foundation Work and Healing

If teeth are being replaced, this is the longest and least visible stage, and its cost depends heavily on how long the gaps have been there. Tan and colleagues' systematic review of post-extraction dimensional changes in humans, published in *Clinical Oral Implants Research*, found horizontal bone loss of 29% to 63% and vertical loss of 11% to 22% within six months of an extraction. A site with too little bone left has to be rebuilt with a [graft](/procedures/periodontal-services/bone-grafting) before an implant can be placed, which adds both a fee and several months of healing that an earlier plan might have avoided. Implants themselves then need time for bone to fuse to the titanium surface, a process typically given three to six months. You are not being kept waiting for no reason during this period — you are waiting for biology, and that wait is a large part of why implants perform as well as they do.

### 4. Provisionals, Then Definitive Restorations

Before the final restorations are made, most substantial reconstructions go through a provisional phase — temporary teeth in the proposed new shape and bite, worn while you eat and speak normally, so that anything uncomfortable is discovered in acrylic rather than in porcelain. This matters most when the bite is being opened, which is technically an increase in occlusal vertical dimension. A 2024 systematic review in the *Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry* [covering 104 publications and 536 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12076109/) found the average evaluation period ran 3.0 ± 2.2 months, with individual cases ranging from about a week to a year. Honesty requires the review's actual conclusion as well: it found no clear evidence that an evaluation period improves the outcome, and concluded that raising the bite can succeed with or without one. It is a widely used safeguard rather than a proven necessity, and it is reasonable to ask why your own plan does or does not include one.

## How Long Does the Whole Thing Take?

Commonly around a year, and not unusually closer to two. Adding up the stages above, a case with no extractions and no implants can be finished in a few months, while a case involving extractions, grafting, implant healing and a provisional phase realistically runs twelve to twenty-four months from first records to final fitting.

The pace is set by healing, not by the dentistry. Grafts, extraction sites and implants each need their own months, and those months cannot be run in parallel with the work that depends on them. This is worth knowing before you compare quotes, because a materially shorter timeline is not automatically a better offer — it usually means either that your case genuinely needs less foundation work, or that a stage has been compressed. The right question is which of those two it is.

There is one advantage buried in the timeline. Because treatment naturally spans more than one calendar year, it usually spans more than one insurance benefit year too, which matters more than most people realize.

## What Drives the Top and the Bottom of the Range?

Four variables account for most of the spread between a $20,000 plan and an $80,000 one. None of them is negotiable in the way people hope, but all of them are worth understanding before you look at a total.

### 1. Whether Teeth Can Be Saved or Must Be Replaced

This is the single largest fork. Restoring a tooth that can be kept means a crown and sometimes a build-up. Replacing it means an extraction, possibly a graft, an implant, months of healing and then a crown on top — several times the cost and several times the time. A plan that saves twelve teeth and a plan that replaces them are both "full-mouth reconstructions" and they are not remotely the same purchase. It is also why delay is expensive: bone volume falls after a tooth is lost, so a site that needed no graft this year may need one later.

### 2. Whether the Bite Has to Be Rebuilt

Where years of wear have shortened the teeth, the reconstruction is not only replacing tooth structure but re-establishing the height at which your jaw closes. That changes the technical difficulty of the case considerably, usually requires the provisional phase described above, and generally means restoring every tooth in both arches rather than only the damaged ones. Cases that leave the bite alone are markedly cheaper than cases that rebuild it.

### 3. Materials and Laboratory Work

Zirconia, layered porcelain and high-grade ceramics cost more than basic materials, and the difference compounds across twenty-four to twenty-eight units. The same is true at arch level, where an acrylic hybrid and a zirconia full-arch bridge can be more than ten thousand dollars apart on the same four implants. Neither is a trick. The costlier option generally resists wear and staining for longer, which is a genuine argument when the restoration is meant to last decades. The [crowns page](/procedures/restoration/crowns-caps) covers how material choice works at the level of a single tooth.

### 4. How Much Gum Disease and Bone Loss Exist Already

Periodontal treatment is a real cost in its own right, ranging from scaling and root planing at the modest end to surgical treatment at the expensive end. Beyond its own fee, it changes everything downstream: bone loss reduces which teeth can be kept, forces grafting before implants, and lengthens the timeline. Two mouths with the same number of failing teeth can be twenty thousand dollars apart on gum health alone.

## What Will Dental Insurance Actually Pay?

Less than the coverage percentages suggest, and the reason is structural rather than unfair. The percentage tells you how a single procedure is reimbursed. The annual maximum tells you when reimbursement stops for the year, and on a plan this size it stops early.

According to the [National Association of Dental Plans](https://www.nadp.org/about-dental-plans-care/understanding-dental-benefits/), about 65% of dental PPOs carry a maximum annual benefit of $1,500 or more, deductibles commonly sit between $50 and $100, and major procedures such as crowns, bridges and dentures are usually reimbursed at the lowest percentage, often around 50%. The same source notes that between 2014 and 2023, on average fewer than 5% of enrollees ever reached their annual maximum in a given year — which is exactly why maximums are set where they are, and exactly why a reconstruction patient hits the ceiling almost immediately.

### Why the Annual Maximum Binds Before the Percentage Does

The arithmetic is worth seeing once. Take a $45,000 plan in which $30,000 of the work is a covered benefit, on a policy with a $1,500 annual maximum and 50% coverage on major services.

| | All in one benefit year | Phased across three benefit years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covered major work billed | $30,000 | $10,000 per year |
| Reimbursed at 50%, capped by the maximum | $1,500 | $1,500 per year |
| Total the plan pays | $1,500 | $4,500 |
| Share of the $45,000 plan | About 3% | About 10% |

That is an illustration built from published plan-design figures rather than a promise about your policy, but the shape of it holds almost universally. Once your covered major work passes roughly $3,000 in a year, the coverage percentage stops mattering and only the maximum does. It also shows why sequencing has financial consequences as well as clinical ones.

### Which Parts of a Plan Insurers Usually Pay Toward

Not every line item is a covered benefit, and the exclusions cluster in exactly the places a reconstruction spends most.

| Element | How dental plans commonly treat it |
| --- | --- |
| Exams, X-rays, cleanings | Preventive — typically covered at or near 100% |
| Fillings and extractions | Basic — commonly a middling percentage, often around 80% in network |
| Crowns, bridges, dentures | Major — commonly the lowest percentage, often around 50% |
| Implant posts | Frequently excluded outright; the crown fitted on top is sometimes covered as a major service |
| Bone grafting and sinus lifts | Often excluded or tightly limited |
| Veneers placed purely for appearance | Generally excluded as cosmetic |

Two practical steps follow from this. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate, sometimes called a predetermination, in which the proposed treatment is submitted to your insurer and the insurer states in writing what it will pay before you commit. And ask whether phasing the plan across benefit years makes clinical sense, because sometimes it does not, and a worse sequence is never worth an extra $1,500.

## How Does a Consultation Actually Produce a Number?

By producing the records first and the number second. A reconstruction quote given before a full examination, imaging and a study of how your jaw closes is not a quote, because the two most expensive variables — which teeth survive and whether the bite has to be rebuilt — cannot be assessed any other way. What you should expect to leave a proper consultation with is a written plan divided into stages, with each stage priced, each stage's purpose explained, and the sequence made explicit.

That document is also what makes second opinions meaningful. Two practices given the same mouth may reach different conclusions about how many teeth are worth saving, and comparing two phased plans side by side shows you exactly where the disagreement is. Comparing two headline totals shows you nothing at all.

If you are preparing for that appointment, these are the questions that surface the differences between plans.

1. Which teeth are you proposing to save, which to remove, and what changes if that judgment is wrong?
2. Is the bite being rebuilt at a new height, and if so, will there be a provisional phase to test it?
3. What is the plan's sequence, and how long is each stage including healing?
4. Which items on this plan are estimates that could move, and what would move them?
5. Is grafting inside the quoted figure or additional to it?
6. Can the plan be phased across benefit years without compromising the result?
7. Will you submit a pre-treatment estimate to my insurer before I commit to anything?

## Is a Full-Mouth Reconstruction Worth Doing All at Once?

Often, yes, though not for the reason people assume. Restoring a mouth as one coordinated plan tends to produce a better result than repairing it tooth by tooth over a decade, because the teeth are designed to work together: the bite is set once, the shade is matched across the whole smile, and each restoration is built to share load with the ones around it. Piecemeal repair tends to mean each new crown is fitted to whatever the mouth has become since the last one, and problems that come from the bite keep reappearing as individual failures.

The honest counterweight is that a reconstruction asks for a larger commitment up front, over a longer period, than most people are expecting when they walk in about a broken tooth. It is also not the only answer. Where budget, bone volume or general health make extensive surgery unwise, [dentures and partial dentures](/procedures/restoration/dentures-partial-dentures) remain a legitimate solution rather than a consolation prize, and a plan can be phased so that the urgent work happens now and the rest happens when it can. What is not sensible is agreeing to a total without seeing what it is made of.
